Appendix A: Instructions

  WELCOME TO THE EXPERIMENT! (Spanish translation)
  This is an experiment to study decision making, so we are not interested in your
particular choices but rather on the individual's average behavior. Thus, all through the
experiment you will be treated anonymously. Neither the experimenters nor the people in
this room will ever know your particular choices. Please do not think that we expect a
particular behavior from you. However, keep in mind that your behavior will affect the
amount of money you can win.
  Next, you will find instructions on the computer screen explaining how the experiment
unfolds. The instructions are the same for all subjects in the laboratory and will be read
aloud by experimenters. Please follow them carefully, as it is important that you
understand the experiment before starting.
  Talking is forbidden during the experiment. If you have any questions, raise your hand
and remain silent. You will be attended to by the experimenters as soon as possible.


  THE EXPERIMENT
  First phase
  The experiment has two phases. In the first one, you are able to get money by solving a
questionnaire.
  The quiz that you will face is the same for all subjects in the room and contains 20
multiple-choice questions with 5 possible answers (only one of them is correct). You
have 35 minutes to solve the quiz. Each of your correct answers will be rewarded at a
reward rate that will be the same for each correct answer but may vary across individuals.
No questions will be rewarded higher than others and the reward of each correct answer
will be randomly announced once you finish the questionnaire. This reward per correct
answer lies between 100 and 200 pesetas and does not depend on your performance.
  You will now receive the questionnaire on a piece of paper. To answer the questions,
you must use the computer screen. Please do not write on the questionnaire, and make
sure that you have selected your answers correctly on the computer screen before
continuing, as the computer will automatically check your answers at the end of this
phase. Calculators cannot be used during the experiment. You will be provided an
additional piece of paper to make computations if needed.
  Remember that during the experiment you are not allowed to communicate with each
other: you can only communicate with the experimenters.

     Second phase
     In this second phase, you will be randomly matched with a subject in this room
and your total earnings will be announced. Remember that the reward of each
correct answer is randomly determined so it does not depend on your performance
in the quiz.
(Subjects were informed about their earnings. They faced a computer screen quite similar
to the one that appears below).
     Now, you will be assigned a type, that is, you will either be player A or player
B. This type is randomly determined to choose the one subject that divides the pie.
Hence, the subject selected as player A will divide the total earnings. Remember
that your choices will be treated anonymously. Neither during the experiment nor
after the experiment will you know the identity of the person you are matched
with.1

Appendix B


   One of the main problems in analyzing the dictator game with production is the
difference in behavior between dictators who follow the theoretical prediction of no
giving and dictators who give some money away. In our experiment, we have 72
observations (i.e., 24 observations in each treatment). We have eight dictators choosing
s=0, one giving s=0.01 and another one transferring s=0.02. All these selfish dictators can
be labeled as "vertical outliers" since these observations have outlying values for the y-
dimension but are not outlying in the x-dimension (e.g., see Figure 1 in the main text).

  From a statistical point of view, the presence of vertical outliers might bias the
evidence in favor of Hypothesis 1, by affecting the LS-estimation. In our paper, we
present the median regression model to deal with this problem.2 The aim of this
supplementary material is to further investigate the validity of our results when
considering other methods, such as the hurdle model or M-estimators.


The hurdle model

  The hurdle model considers the process by which some dictator decides to transfer zero
or some amount to the recipient as separate from the process by which the dictator
decides how much to give away. The specification therefore assumes that dictators have
to decide whether to transfer any amount at all, and only then does the process
determining the positive giving apply.

   In our case, this specification might be useful to investigate how the treatment
conditions and the recipients' contribution affect dictators' decision about (i) whether to
transfer some money or not and (ii) how much money to transfer. The hurdle model is
particularly useful when the data presents a "spike" that needs to be addressed explicitly.
In a recent paper, Botelho et al. (2009) apply this model in public good experiments (in
which the zero contribution is expected) to investigate the equivalence between the
"random strangers" design (in which subjects are randomly paired with others in the
session) and the "perfect strangers" design (in which subjects are guaranteed to meet only
once).

   Theoretically, the likelihood function for the overall hurdle model is constructed as the
product of two likelihoods. The first one uses a standard probit specification defined over
an index function ziλ, where λ is a parameter vector to be estimated and zi is a vector of
explanatory variables which contains in our case information on the treatments and the
recipient's contribution to the surplus. The second component in the hurdle model is the
conditional likelihood that the dictator gives a certain fraction of the surplus away. Since
the dependent variable in our model is the proportion of the surplus that dictators give
away, this likelihood function can be constructed by using the specification developed by
Papke and Wooldridge (1996) for fractional dependent variables. Thus the log-likelihood
of observation i can be defined as li(μ)=si ×log[Φ(ziμ)]+(1-si)×log[1-Φ(ziμ)] for
transferred proportion si, parameter vector μ, and the normal cumulative distribution Φ(⋅).
Thus the overall likelihood function for the hurdle model requires the estimation of λ and
μ. We report the maximum likelihood estimates of the hurdle model in Table A, where
DB and BL are defined as two dummy variables which take the value 1 in the DB and BL
treatment, and are 0 otherwise.

    We see in Table A that dictators' decision about giving zero or something is not
 affected by the treatment or the recipients' contribution. These variables, however, do
 have a statistically significant effect on the proportion of the surplus that dictators give
 away. Thus, if we compare dictators' giving in the DW and the DB treatment, we see that
 when dictators give money away, they significantly increase the proportion of the surplus
 to be given in the DB treatment (p-value=0.026), and this occur regardless of what
 recipients have contributed. In addition, the weight that dictators assign to the
 accountability principle significantly decreases in the DB treatment, whereas the weight
 that is assigned to the libertarian principle significantly increases (p-values=0.010 and
 0.009 respectively). As a result, we easily reject the null hypothesis that dictators behave
 the same in the DW and the DB treatment (χ₃²=7.63, with p-value=0.0543).


 M-estimators

    As commented above, observations on the horizontal line s=0 correspond to selfish
 dictators and can be considered vertical outliers, especially when we are using the linear
 regression to test the theoretical prediction of justice (45 degree line). Rousseeuw and
 Leroy (2003) argue that the median regression presented in our paper accomplish robust
 estimates in this case. However, a generalization of the median regression to a wider class
 of estimators, called M-estimators, can be considered. Assume the linear specification si =
 θ X + ei and let ri = si - ŝ i denotes the estimated residual. Then, the M-estimator can be
 defined as:
                                                 
                                                θ M = argmin θ ∑ni=1 ρ(ri(θ)/σ)

   where ρ(⋅) is a loss function and σ is a measure of dispersion to standardize residuals
 and guarantee independence with respect to the measurement units of the dependent
 variable. One of the most popular loss functions is the Tukey Biweight function, which
 down-weights the observations that deviate from the majority of the data and is about

95% as efficient as OLS.3 In Table B, we report the estimates of the linear regression

Appendix C


In Table 3 we investigate the validity of our conjecture by considering separately
dictators who have contributed more or less than recipients. Our aim is to test whether
higher monetary contributions yield dictators to follow the libertarian principle more
frequently, as this principle bases on subjects’ monetary contributions. The case in which
dictators contribute less than recipients (and they have also fewer questions correctly)
imply that the egalitarian principle (which predict a 50-50 division) “dominates” both the
libertarian and the accountability principle, because it yields higher payoffs to the
dictator.4 This latter scenario clearly represents a “nice” framework to compare the 50-50
division versus the criteria based on “earnings”, but some problems might arise in other
cases (e.g., if dictators accumulate more money than recipients but higher monetary
contributions are due to smaller reward levels, Is it better to for dictators to divide
according to the accountability principle or according to the libertarian one?).
In Figure 1a in the main text we can see that the accountability principle (AP) coincide
with the egalitarian principle (EP) when the recipient and the dictator has the same
number of correct answers (xa=0.5). In Figure 1b we see that the libertarian principle (LP)
and the EP coincide when recipients’ contribution is exactly half of the surplus (xl=0.5).
If we put these two figures together (e.g., if we plot the recipients’ correct answers xa in
the horizontal axis), we will see that the AP always dominates the libertarian principle
(LP) in the DW treatment because for any contribution (xa) on the horizontal axis, the AP
is always below the LP (i.e., it is better for the dictator to divide the surplus according to
effort instead of using the monetary contribution). This is always true in the DW
treatment as illustrated in the figure below. However, the egalitarian principle (EP) is the
best option from the dictator’s point of view in this treatment if xa ≥ 0.5 (i.e., if the
recipient has more than half of correct answers, the “best” solution is to divide the surplus
in two identical parts). We can do exactly the same for all the three treatments under
consideration. We will see that the LP always “dominates” the AP in the DB treatment,
but it only “dominates” the egalitarian principle if the percentage of recipient’s correct
answers is smaller than q1=0.6. If xa ≥ q1 = 0.6, then the EP will dominate the remaining
ones in the DB treatment.5 In the BL treatment, the LP and AP coincide.

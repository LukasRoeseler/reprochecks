Barreca\_JournPoliEco\_2016\_J999
---------------------------------

This is the single-trace claim selected from this paper in Phase 1. It
includes two levels of claim 3: 3a, a statement of the finding, and 3b,
the specification of the test. In bushel-trace claims, claim 3b is not
coded, and claim 3 is analagous to the single-trace 3a.

-   **Claim 2 (Abstract statement):** …the mortality impact of days with
    mean temperature exceeding 807F declined by 75 percent. Almost the
    entire decline occurred after 1960.

-   **Claim 3a (Finding statement):** The hypothesis is that the
    coefficients on the interaction terms will be negative at the
    extreme temperature categories. A negative coefficient would be
    interpreted as evidence that the diffusion of a particular modifier
    reduced a population’s vulnerability to temperature extremes,
    relative to the modifier’s effect on the mortality impact of days in
    the 60F–69F range. In particular, the modifier variables are
    expected to play a key role in dampening the mortality effects of
    high temperatures, e.g.,days &gt;90F. In light of the sampling
    errors, it is apparent that we cannot reject that the widespread
    adoption of residential air conditioning explains the entire
    reduction in hot day mortality.

-   **Claim 3b (Test specification):** Table 6 presents the results from
    the fitting of several versions of equation 2. Equation 2 is
    estimated using generalized least squares (GLS), where the weights
    correspond to the square root of the contemporaneous state
    population. The dependent variable is log monthly mortality rate and
    the focal independent variable is “number of days above 90F x share
    of residential AC”, for the sample from 1960-2004.

-   **Claim 4 (Results statement):** Table 6 presents the results from
    the fitting of several versions of equation 2. Panel B indicates
    that the diffusion of residential air conditioning is associated
    with a sizable and statistically significant decrease in mortality
    due to hot days in the 1960-2004 period. The estimates in columns 2b
    and 3b suggest that each 10 percentage point increase in residential
    AC ownership is associated with a decrease in the mortality effect
    of &gt;90F days by 0.0021 log points; this is roughly 10 percent of
    the effect of a &gt;90F day in the pre-1960 period. In Table 6, the
    coefficient of “number of days above 90F x share of residential AC”
    is -0.0212 with a standard error of 0.0054 and a p-value &lt; 0.001.

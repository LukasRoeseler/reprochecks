A comment on "A practical signiﬁcance bias in laypeople’s evaluation of scientiﬁc 
ﬁndings"
Jan Philipp Röer, Thomas Ostermann, and Sebastian Appelbaum
Witten/Herdecke University, Department of Psychology and Psychotherapy, Witten, 
Germany
Corresponding Author: Jan Philipp Röer, jan.roeer@uni-wh.de
--- PAGE 2 ---
Abstract
We tested the computational reproducibility of a research claim from Michal and Shah 
(2024) according to which individuals assume that a scientiﬁc ﬁnding described in a media 
report is meaningfully large when numerical information about the magnitude of this ﬁnding 
is missing. This reﬂects the following statement from the paper's abstract: "Participants 
who viewed interventions with unreported effect magnitudes were more likely to endorse 
interventions compared with those who viewed interventions with small effects and were 
just as likely to endorse interventions as those who viewed interventions with large effects, 
suggesting a practical signiﬁcance bias.". Using the dataset from the original study, we 
successfully reproduced the focal test of the paper for both studies. We uncovered a 
couple of errors in the process, which did not change the studies’ main results. This 
reproduction was conducted during the replication games workshop at the META-REP 
Conference 2024 in Munich, Germany.

1 Introduction
The to-be-reproduced paper (Michal & Shah, 2024) deals with the question how people 
estimate the size of effects they read about in the media and what inﬂuence this has on 
everyday decisions. Speciﬁcally, the authors investigated whether numerical information 
about the magnitude of a ﬁnding helps people to arrive at better decisions. Two studies 
were conducted to test this. In both studies, participants were randomly assigned to one of 
four different conditions (No effect size information, Large effect, Small effect no prompt, 
Small effect with prompt). In Study 1, participants read an article describing a new math 
curriculum to boost elementary students’ math proﬁciency. In Study 2, participants read an 
article about a new energy bar with beneﬁcial effects on mental alertness.
1.1 Outcome Measures
In both studies, participants rated the meaningfulness of the improvement from the 
intervention in relation to its cost on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 100 (very much). In Study 
1, participants were asked to what extent they would recommend the new math curriculum 
on a scale from 0 (do not recommend) to 100 (strongly recommend). In Study 2, 
participants rated how likely they were to purchase the new energy bar (Study 2) on a 
scale from 0 (very unlikely) to 100 (very likely). Participants also completed the 
Abbreviated Numeracy Scale (Weller et al., 2013).
1.2 Focal Test
After controlling for numeracy, decision ratings should be higher for participants in the no 
effect size information condition compared to the small effect no prompt condition and 
decision ratings should not differ between the no effect size information condition and the 
large effect condition.

1.3 Reproducibility criteria
The statistical analysis was the same as in the original study. We ran an analysis of 
variance (ANOVA) using a regression model with decision rating as the dependent 
variable, numeracy as a continuous covariate, and condition as a between-subjects factor 
to reproduce the focal test.
2 Computational Reproducibility Results
For the computational reproduction, we used the dataset from the original study retrieved 
from the OSF project page at https://osf.io/4927f/. The raw data were provided, but no 
cleaning or analysis code.

information“  and “Large effect”, while there were slight differences in the “Small effect no 
prompt” and “Small effect with prompt” groups (Table 1.1). We highlighted the 
discrepancies between the calculated values and the values reported in the paper in

3C o n c l u s i o n
During the replication games workshop at the META-REP Conference 2024, we have 
dedicated an entire day to computationally reproduce the focal tests for Study 1 and Study 
2 from Michal and Shah (2024). We are happy to report that the reproduction was largely 
successful in that we only found minor to moderate errors that do not challenge the 
authors’ core conclusions. Altogether, the scope and implications of these errors do not 
seem to necessitate a correction of the article. 
We believe that some deviations could have been avoided if the authors—unless we have 
overlooked something—had reported how exactly the mean differences were calculated 
(2.1.3 and 2.2.3). For the sake of transparency and openness, it would be ideal if this 
information were available in order to determine whether the results can be reproduced 
independently using the same method. Other minor errors we have found are missing 
signs leading to incorrect conﬁdence intervals (2.2.3) and incorrect values in the text 
("Study 1 no prompt M = 45.26; Study 2 no prompt M = 34.98; Study 2 with prompt M = 
45.22; Study 2 with prompt M = 31.68“, p. 320).
Furthermore, when reviewing the OSF registrations, we noticed that two different pre-
analysis plans were uploaded for Study 1, which deviate in three out of four hypotheses 
(2.3). A major advantage of preregistration is that it can be made transparent which 
hypotheses and analyses were determined by the researchers prior to data collection and 
which were determined by the data (Lakens, 2024; Willroth & Atherton, 2024; see also 
Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven & Mellor, 2018). The authors state that the second pre-
analysis plan was published prior to any human observation of the data. We have no 
--- PAGE 12 ---
reason to doubt that the information provided by the authors is correct, but ideally all 
deviations from the pre-analysis plan should be transparently reported, especially if the 
changes are somewhat extensive and are published after the data collection has already 
been completed.

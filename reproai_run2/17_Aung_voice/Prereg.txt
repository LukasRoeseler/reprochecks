Pre-registration 
The acoustic properties of vertebrate vocalizations are often sexually dimorphic and 
show evidence of having been shaped by sexual selection (Charlton & Reby, 2016; Ryan et al., 
1990). Sex differences in fundamental frequency (fo, the rate of vocal fold vibration during 
phonation) derive from differences in the length and thickness of the vocal folds (Titze, 2000). 
Larger vocal folds with less muscular tension vibrate at a lower rate, which is perceived as lower 
pitch (Titze, 2000). Age-related changes in decreased fo have been observed in men and 
women; however, the pubertal rise in testosterone causes the vocal folds to enlarge and for fo to 
drop significantly in men (Fouquet et al., 2016; Harries et al., 1998; Markova et al., 2016). 
Consequently, compared to women, men have approximately 60% longer vocal folds and an fo 
that is five standard deviations lower (Puts et al., 2016). In many anthropoid primates, low fo 
appears to evolve in males in response to mating competition (Puts et al., 2016), perhaps 
because it exaggerates perceived body size (Delgado, 2006; Puts et al., 2016; Rendall et al., 
2007) and hence conveys the appearance of dominance and formidability (Darwin, 1871; Puts 
et al., 2016). In humans, lower male fo has been found to increase perceptions of height, 
dominance, and fighting ability, suggesting that it may be a signal of formidability and/or 
dominance (Puts et al., 2006; Puts et al., 2007; Wolff & Puts, 2010). In female voices, higher fo 
has been found to increase perceptions of attractiveness (Borkowska & Pawlowski, 2011; 
Fraccaro et al., 2011; Puts et al., 2012). While many studies have shown that fo influences 
social perceptions, it is unknown (a) how variable these effects are across societies and (b) the 
degree to which they depend on theoretically relevant social and societal variables. The current 
study addresses these questions by manipulating fo in men’s and women’s voices and 
examining 1) mating competition-relevant perceptions of men’s and women’s fo across contexts 
and cultures and 2) environmental factors that may predict cross-cultural variation in 
perceptions of men’s and women’s fo. Below, we outline our hypotheses, method, and analysis

Research Objectives 
Masculinized and feminized voice preferences vary across contexts 
 Evidence across primates, including humans, suggests that sex differences in voice 
pitch likely evolved in response to the intensity of mating competition (Puts et al., 2016). In 
humans, male fo has been shown to negatively predict other men’s perceptions of prestige 
and/or dominance and women’s perceptions of attractiveness, and the effect of male fo appears 
to be stronger on dominance than prestige or attractiveness (Cheng et al., 2016; Puts et al., 
2007; Saxton et al., 2016; Rosenfield et al., 2020), and stronger on attractiveness ratings for a 
short-term purely sexual relationship, such as a one night stand (short-term attractiveness), than 
a long-term committed relationship (long-term attractiveness) by women (Hodges-Simeon et al., 
2011; Puts, 2005).  
 It is also possible that sexual dimorphism in voice pitch is due to sexual selection 
favoring higher pitch in women’s voices as a means of mate attraction. If so, and if men 
generally prefer higher voice pitch in women, then women may perceive high-pitched feminine 
voices as indicating a greater threat in competition for mates (Puts et al. 2011; Zhang, 2016). In 
female voices, fo has been shown to positively predict other women’s perceptions of 
flirtatiousness and attractiveness to men and men’s perceptions of attractiveness (Borkowska & 
Pawlowski, 2011; Fraccaro et al., 2011; Puts et al., 2011; Puts et al., 2012).   
In general, fo appears to be utilized in solving recurrent adaptive problems that would 
have been present in our ancestral conditions (Tooby & Cosmides, 1992; Barrett & Kurzban, 
2006), especially the problems of assessing the formidability and threat potential of competitors 
for mates, and of choosing high-quality mates. Here, we aim to explore the functional design of 
fo in humans by examining how masculinized and feminized fo influences evaluations of same-
sex competitors and potential mates.

Perceptions of domestic violence and danger toward children 
 Among women, perceived domestic violence has been shown to predict decreased 
preferences for masculine faces (Borras-Guevara et al., 2017a; Li et al., 2014) and voices (Li et 
al., 2014). Increased perceptions that men are dangerous to their children has also been shown 
to reduce women’s facial masculinity preferences (Borras-Guevara et al., 2017b). Hence, we 
will test whether increased perceptions of domestic violence toward women and children reduce 
women’s preferences for lower male fo on short-term and long-term attractiveness. We do not 
expect similar associations with male preferences for female fo.  
Cross-cultural factors: health, inequality, food security  
 Given the influence of environmental variables on masculinity preferences, sex 
differences in perceptions of male and female fo may vary across societies. Previously, cross-
cultural explanations in preferences for facial masculinity, but not vocal masculinity, have been 
reported. Findings are mixed; whereas some studies (Brooks et al., 2011; Little, DeBruine, & 
Jones, 2013; Scott et al., 2014) reported that both preferences and saliency of sexually 
dimorphic faces are strongest in societies swith favorable ecological conditions (higher national 
health and development indices) or increased intra-sexual competition (higher homicide rates 
and income inequality), others (DeBruine et al., 2013; DeBruine, Jones, Little, Crawford, & 
Welling, 2011) suggest that female preferences for facial masculinity are stronger in nations with 
lower health and higher levels of pathogens. The former studies link the advantage of 
masculinized traits to obtaining greater material benefits and distinctive signaling toward other 
potential mates and rivals, whereas the latter studies link such preference to genetic benefits of 
preferring masculinized traits, especially in environments where offspring survival is more 
dependent on pathogen resistance. A recent study by Marcinkowska et al. (2019) more strongly 
supported the former hypothesis, reporting that female preferences for facial masculinity were 
stronger in nations with better health and developmental indices. We will therefore explore

Method 
Participants 
Data have already been collected in person from participants in 22 different countries, n = 3,163 
(1,614 female). Participants were either from university populations (n = 1472) or community 
samples (n = 1692). Participants’ ages ranged from 12 – 96 years old. Participants reported 
their relationship status; number of living children; sexual orientation (a 5-point Kinsey scale); 
primary language; location where they were raised, and whether they could hear the recordings 
clearly, understand the recordings, and hear a difference between the two voices. We aimed to 
collect at least 32 males and 32 females from each sampling location, but this was not possible 
in all cases, and in some locations many more were feasible (Fig 2).

Stimulus Selection, Manipulation, and Pairing 
 Several critical methodological decisions pertained to the selection of acoustic stimuli. In 
order to increase ecological validity by capturing some of the prosody of spoken language, we 
utilized a segment of continuous speech, rather than less natural options, such as using isolated 
vowel sounds. Specifically, we used the speech segment, “They act as a prism and form a 
rainbow,” from the first sentence of the emotionally neutral “rainbow passage” (Fairbanks, 
1960). We chose this brief text in order to minimize rater fatigue, and because previous 
research has shown that social judgments can be made from very short voice recordings 
(Feinberg et al., 2005; Pisanski et al., 2012; Sorokowski et al., 2019). Another critical 
methodological decision pertained to whether to utilize stimuli recorded in the local languages of 
each sampling location, or to use the same stimuli for all raters regardless of sampling location. 
We opted to use identical stimuli across all participants in order to increase internal validity, and 
to allow us to explore possible effects of cultural factors on perceptions that were not 
confounded with stimulus language.  
Two male and two female voice clips of the first sentence of the rainbow passage 
(Fairbanks, 1960) were selected from a sample of voice recordings collected at Michigan State 
University (Puts et al., 2016). These voices were selected because their mean fo was within 1SD 
of the mean, Pf  (the average standardized formant value for the first 4 formants; Puts et al. 
2012) was within 1SD of the mean, there were no errors in the first sentence of the Rainbow 
Passage, and they sounded natural to the experimenters when pitch was manipulated by 2SD 
in both directions. Additionally, only voices of women who were not taking hormonal 
contraception were chosen for female voice clips.  
We made five pitch manipulations to each of the four voices. Using Pratt software and 
the means from the voice samples, each voice was manipulated to the mean pitch (Females: 
5.881 ERB, Males: 3.687 ERB), to 1SD below and above the mean pitch (Females: 0.339 ERB, 
Males: 0.280 ERB), and to 2 SD below and above the mean pitch (Females: 0.678 ERB, Males: 
=====PAGE 8=====

0.559 ERB). Manipulation by ERB produces shifts of the same perceptual magnitude regardless 
of starting pitch. Unmanipulated voice clips were used as the starting point for each 
manipulation so that each stimulus was the result of only one manipulation. Using the software 
Audacity, the voice clips were cut to the 2nd phrase, “They act as a prism and form a rainbow”.   
 Stimuli from each voice were then combined into 3 types of pairs, such that all stimulus 
pairs were 2SD apart: 2SD feminized pitch paired with mean pitch, 2SD masculinized pitch 
paired with mean pitch, and 1SD feminized pitch paired with 1SD masculinized pitch. These 
stimulus pairs allowed us to assess linear and curvilinear effects of voice pitch on perceptions. 
For example, if women prefer average male voices over deeper voices, then they should exhibit 
little preference when listening to 1SD feminized vs. 1SD masculinized pitch pairs, but they 
should tend to prefer mean pitch over 2SD feminized pitch and 2SD masculinized pitch. 
Procedure 
 Demographic data were collected from all participants. For some countries, participants 
also answered 7-item questions relating to perceived vulnerability to domestic violence against 
men, women, and children (Borras-Guevara et al., 2017a). Participants then listened to pairs of 
voices and were asked to choose one voice from each pair (first or second) in answer to specific 
questions. Men were asked to judge male voices on which voice in each pair sounded more 
respected, admired, talented and successful [“prestige”], and which voice in each pair sounded 
more likely to win a physical fight [“dominance”]. Men were also asked to judge female voices 
on which voice in each pair sounded more attractive for a short-term, uncommitted romantic 
relationship [“short-term”], and which voice was more attractive for a long-term committed 
relationship such as steady dating or marriage [“long-term”]. Women were asked to judge 
female voices on which voice in each pair would be more attractive to men [“attractive”], and 
which voice in each pair sounded more interested in attracting men [“flirtatious”]. They were 
asked to judge male voices on which voice in each pair sounded more attractive for a short-
term, uncommitted romantic relationship [“short-term”], and which voice was more attractive for 
=====PAGE 9=====

a long-term committed relationship such as steady dating or marriage [“long-term”]. The 
dependent variables were coded as either 0 (feminized voice chosen) or 1 (masculinized voice 
chosen). Each question of the 4 sex-specific questions was asked 6 times (2 voices × 3 
manipulation pairs), and thus each participant rated 24 pairs of voices. Due to 
miscommunication, Malagasy (Madagascar) participants listened to only one randomly assigned 
pair of voices per question, and thus only rated 4 pairs of voices in total.  
 Voice stimuli were presented using iPods and Sennheiser HD280 Pro headphones in all 
our samples. We counterbalanced stimuli via two iTunes playlists for each sex. Each playlist 
featured 6 pairs of voices, with 3 pairs from each stimuli voice. Two pairs featured mean pitch 
first, two pairs featured masculinized pitch first, and two pairs featured feminized pitch first. The 
two playlists for each sex featured reversed pairs (i.e. If the 1SD masculinized version of a voice 
was presented before the 1SD feminized version in the first album, the feminized version would 
be presented first in the second album). The order of pairs of each album was randomized using 
the “shuffle” function on iPods. Approximately half of participants listened to album 1 of each 
sex and half of participants listened to album 2 of each sex.  
 In addition to counterbalancing pairs of voices with the use of multiple albums, male and 
female albums and the order of questions about the stimuli were also counterbalanced. The one 
exception was that male and female albums were always alternated in order to reduce rater 
fatigue and provide greater independence between responses to the two questions about each 
sex of speaker. That is, no participant listened and answered questions about one sex’s voices 
twice in a row — participants either heard albums in the order of male-female-male-female or 
female-male-female-male. This created a total of 16 different respondent sheets for each sex in 
addition to randomized order of the presentation of stimuli. 
Cross-cultural Variables 
 Participants might prefer voices closer to the local average pitch. To test this possibility, 
we aim to collect voice recordings from 20 participants (10 males and 10 females), speaking

recordings will be primarily obtained from the localingual website (Ding, 2017), an interactive 
platform where participants from various countries can voluntarily contribute their audio 
recordings. Using Praat software, mean pitch of each sex will be extracted across individuals for 
each country and used as a predictor.

replacement for missing data of each variable. As in Marcinkowska et al. (2019), we expected to 
obtain two extracted variables: health/development & Inequality factors.

Analysis Plan 
 Analyses will be conducted using Bayesian multilevel models via the statistical software 
R (R Core Team, 2016), ape package (Paradis E. & Schliep, 2018), and brms package 
(Bürkner, 2017). As mating competition is most likely to be relevant to the ages between 16 and 
40, we limit our analyses to this age range during hypothesis testing. Main hypotheses will be 
tested using logistic mixed effects models, where choices (either 0 or 1) will be entered as 
=====PAGE 11=====
MATING RELEVANT PERCEPTIONS OF VOICE PITCH                                                                                  11 
 
dependent variables. To test whether participants’ preferences for masculinized and feminized 
voices across contexts, vocalizer sex (male, or female voices), question types (e.g., prestige, 
dominant, short-term, long-term), and stimulus types (3 stimulus pairs) will be entered as fixed 
effects. In a separate model, participants’ age, relationship status, number of children, and 
sexual orientation will be entered as fixed effects to test whether participants’ demographic 
characteristics influence their preferences for masculinized and feminized voices across 
questions. To test whether perceived domestic violence risk shapes men’s and women’s voice 
preferences for the opposite sex in short-term and long-term mating contexts, we will run two 
separate models where question types, stimulus types, and perceived domestic violence ratings 
toward women, men, and children will be entered as fixed effects. As in Marcinkowska et al. 
(2019), independent factor analysis will be used to extract unique country-level factors to 
account for highly inter-correlated cross-cultural variables and we will use these extracted 
factors, as well as the mean pitch of the local language, to predict preferences for masculinized 
and feminized voices across each question in our study. Across all models, the potential non-
independence of observations made by the same participant, participants in the same country, 
and the use of same stimuli across participants will be accounted for by including participant ID, 
country, and voice ID as random intercepts. Geographical region will be included as an 
additional random effect to account for potential non-independence between countries (e.g., 
similar climate, shared cultural history, etc.). In addition to geographical relatedness between 
nations, we will also control for the phylogenetic relatedness between nations using linguistic 
distance matrix. Random slopes will be specified maximally, as recommended by (Barr et al., 
2013).

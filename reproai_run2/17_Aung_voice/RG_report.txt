A comment on “Effects of Voice Pitch on Social Perceptions Vary With Relational 
Mobility and Homicide Rate” 
 
Abstract 
The study by Aung et al. (2024) analysed the influence of voice’s fundamental frequency 
(fo) on perceptions of individual traits such as attractiveness, prestige and formidability. 
Authors highlight the influence of the socioecological factors of relational mobility and 
violence. Using data from 22 countries and Bayesian mixed effect models, authors report 
that low male fo are associated with higher perceptions of formidability and prestige in 
contexts of high mobility and homicide rates. The study  shows that high female fo is 
related to women’s perceptions of flirtatiousness in contexts of lower relational mobility 
where competition for mates is larger. We successfully reproduced the main findings of 
the study and performed an analysis using an alternative frequentist approach.  
 
1. Introduction 
Data were obtained from 3173 participants in 44 locations across 22 nations. Participants 
listened to pairs of voices and were asked to choose one voice from each pair (first or 
second) in response to specific questions. Men were asked to indicate which male voice 
in each pair sounded more respected, admired, talented, and successful (“prestige”) and 
which male voice in each pair sounded more likely to win a physical fight (“formidability”). 
Men were also asked to indicate which female voice in each pair sounded more attractive 
for a short-term, uncommitted romantic relationship (“short term”) and which female voice 
in each pair was more attractive for a long -term, committed relationship such as steady 
dating or marriage (“long term”). Women were asked to in dicate which female voice in 
each pair would be more attractive to men (“attractive”) and which female voice in each 
pair sounded more interested in attracting men (“flirtatious”). Women were also asked to 
indicate which male voice in each pair sounded mor e attractive for a short -term, 
uncommitted romantic relationship (“short term”) and which male voice in each pair was 
more attractive for a long-term, committed relationship such as steady dating or marriage 
(“long term”). For the data analysis, binary logistic Bayesian mixed -effects models were 
fit to the data using RStan via the brms package. 
=====PAGE 2=====
In the present report , we test whether the main results and claims made by the 
authors are computationally reproducible. We focus on the analysis of the impact of the 
fundamental frequency ( fo) on several indicators of perceptions of female and male 
voices, as reported in the text (pp. 255-257).  
 
2. Computational Reproducibility: 
We used the replication package provided by the authors here: https://osf.io/tnygr/. The 
replication package lacked a README file to guide readers. The authors provide raw 
data and distance matrices, as well as analysis data that results from running the 
Bayesian models. Very minimal cleaning code is used , but this can be explained by the 
experimental nature of this study and cannot be deemed a limitation.   
Authors specify in the text particular versions of R packages used for analysis . We 
tried using the same versions; some of them were hard to access and install. Running the 
models was ultimately successful, but a lot of set-up and troubleshooting was necessary 
on some of our machines (we used both Windows and macOS devices).  
We noticed a minor error when checking the code. At the start of the main code file 
(“PsychSci.R”), data is imported as a data.frame object called “data”; however, the 
subsequent model uses an object called “newdata”, which is never defined anywhere 
else. We believe that “newdata” was actually supposed to be “data”, and making this 
change solved the issue.  
The authors provided code for over 70 models that they ran . We chose to focus on 
models M1, M17, M18, M19, M20, M23, M57, M58, and M62 , since they seemed to 
constitute the most significant part of the authors’ claims . We selected these models as 
opposed to reproducing all models due to computational and time constraints. We 
compared models that resulted from the code and analysis data provided to find whether 
they were equal. Despite some factors  that made our reproduction more cumbersome,  
we ultimately consider that the authors fulfilled all the reproducibility criteria (see Table 
1).

Authors reported coefficients, estimated marginal means and post-hoc comparisons. 
Results reported in text are a mix of beta (b) coefficients and odds ratios (OR); however, 
the labels were often incorrect. This made the comparison of results in text and those 
obtained when running the models confusing and time -consuming. Part of the  b 
coefficients reported in the text are actually odds ratios. This has been checked by  
exponentiating the coefficients from the model output (eb) and comparing them to those 
reported in the article (see Table 2). While in those instances the coefficients match after 
exponentiating, many of the credible intervals (CrI) still don’t match – we are not sure why 
that is. The differences are small and don’t affect the claims made by the authors, but 
they go beyond what could be explained by rounding error. We also noticed some very 
minor inconsistencies that we suspect come from at times truncating and at times 
rounding up results to three or two decimals. 
 
3. Discrepancies between pre-registered report and article:  
While we appreciate the authors' commitment to pre -registration and open -science 
practices, we observed a possible shift in emphasis between the pre-registration form and 
the final manuscript. For instance, the pre -registered report included health, inequa lity, 
and food security as key factors, whereas the final manuscript focuses primarily on the 
=====PAGE 4=====
role of relational mobility and male rivalry without linking the two to the key factors 
highlighted in the pre-registered report.  
  
4. Findings on the moderating role of relational mobility  
The authors did not report findings related to the moderating roles of male rivalry or 
relational mobility. Our analysis indicates that relational mobility does not moderate 
women's preference for lower male voice pitch. Instead, women generally prefer a higher 
male pitch across varying degrees of male rivalry and relational mobility.  A higher male 
voice pitch (fo) slightly increased the probability of short-term or long-term partner choice 
judgments across nations, contrary to theoretical expectations. 
All in all, the reported results show a slight increase in the probability of short -term 
partner choice judgments for higher male voice pitch, contrary to theoretical expectations. 
No strong evidence was found linking pitch to long-term mate preferences, suggesting a 
minimal role for pitch in shaping preferences across diverse cultures. 
5. Robustness reproduction: 
As a robustness check, we tried to reproduce the main findings of this paper using a 
frequentist modelling approach. We used logistic regression mixed models and included 
all the variables used by the authors, except for geographic relatedness.  Results can be 
found in the replication table . Overall, the results from the frequentist models are very 
similar to the results from the Bayesian models and  do not change  the interpretation 
advanced by the authors (see Table 2). 
6. Conclusions:  
In this brief report, we presented our findings after attempting to replicate the results of 
the original authors and implementing an additional robustness check. We find that the 
main findings could be reproduced successfully and that they hold even when using a 
modelling approach based on the frequentist framework. While we did find some issues 
related to the code provided, the reporting of the coefficients, and some inconsistencies 
between the preregistration and the article, they were minor  and did not clash with th e 
overall narrative of the paper.

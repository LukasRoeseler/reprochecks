Clustering of health, crime and social-welfare
inequality in 4 million citizens from two nations
Leah S. Richmond-Rakerd 1,2*, Stephanie D’Souza 3, Signe Hald Andersen 4, Sean Hogan5,
Renate M. Houts1, Richie Poulton 5, Sandhya Ramrakha5, Avshalom Caspi1,6,7,8,9, Barry J. Milne3,9 and
Terrie E. Moffitt 1,6,7,8,9
Health and social scientists have documented the hospital revolving-door problem, the concentration of crime, and long-term
welfare dependence. Have these distinct fields identified the same citizens? Using administrative databases linked to 1.7 mil-
lion New Zealanders, we quantified and monetized inequality in distributions of health and social problems and tested whether
they aggregate within individuals. Marked inequality was observed: Gini coefficients equalled 0.96 for criminal convictions,
0.91 for public-hospital nights, 0.86 for welfare benefits, 0.74 for prescription-drug fills and 0.54 for injury-insurance claims.
Marked aggregation was uncovered: a small population segment accounted for a disproportionate share of use-events and
costs across multiple sectors. These findings were replicated in 2.3 million Danes. We then integrated the New Zealand data-
bases with the four-decade-long Dunedin Study. The high-need/high-cost population segment experienced early-life factors
that reduce workforce readiness, including low education and poor mental health. In midlife they reported low life satisfac-
tion. Investing in young people’s education and training potential could reduce health and social inequalities and enhance
population wellbeing.

Study description Quantitative analysis of data collected in 2 population-based administrative registers and 1 prospective cohort study.
Research sample Data were drawn from 3 studies. Participants in our study population were drawn from the Statistics New Zealand Integrated Data
Infrastructure (NZIDI), a collection of de-identified, whole-of-population administrative data sources linked at the individual level by a
common spine. The eligible population for analyses was defined as all individuals who were (a) born in New Zealand between 1950-1984,
and (b) resided in the country for any period of time between the July 2006-June 2016 fiscal years (N=1,711,590; approximately age
22-66 years). We divided the study population into seven four-year age-bands.
Participants in our replication population were drawn from Danish nationwide administrative registers. The replication population
covered the same birth years and 10-year observation period as the NZIDI study population (N=2,363,240).
Participants in the third sample were drawn from the Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study, which tracks the
development of 1,037 individuals born in 1972-1973 in Dunedin, New Zealand. Included in the present report were data from 940 Study
members (93.3% of the 1,007 participants alive at the phase-38 assessment, when administrative-record linkage was performed).
Sampling strategy We analyzed data from all individuals for whom administrative-record data were available during the observation period.
Data collection NZIDI and Danish register data were collected from nationwide administrative databases. Dunedin Study data were collected through in-
person interviews and matching to nationwide administrative databases.
Timing The observation period for the NZIDI and Danish administrative registers ranged from July 2006 to June 2016. Matching to administrative
databases in the Dunedin Study was completed following the end of the age-38 assessment, which had an end date of March 31, 2012.
Data exclusions In the NZIDI and Danish registers, we excluded individuals who had evidence of a death prior to the observation period, an overseas spell
spanning the entire observation period, or no evidence of residence in the respective countries during the observation period.
In the Dunedin Study sample, we excluded participants who were deceased prior to the study period, who did not reside in New Zealand
during the study period, who were long-term missing, or who did not provide consent for administrative-record searches at the age-38
assessment.
Non-participation NZIDI and Danish registers: not applicable, as they entailed secondary data analysis of routinely-collected population-level data.
Dunedin Study sample: 35 Study members (3.5% of the 1,007 participants alive at the phase-38 assessment) were long-term missing or
did not provide consent for administrative-record searches.
Randomization Participants were not allocated into experimental groups.

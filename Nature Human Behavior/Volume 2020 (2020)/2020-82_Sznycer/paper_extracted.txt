The origins of criminal law
Daniel Sznycer 1 ✉ and Carlton Patrick 2
Laws against wrongdoing may originate in justice intuitions that are part of universal human nature, according to the adapta-
tionist theory of the origins of criminal law. This theory proposes that laws can be traced to neurocognitive mechanisms and
ancestral selection pressures. According to this theory, laypeople can intuitively recreate the laws of familiar and unfamiliar
cultures, even when they lack the relevant explicit knowledge. Here, to evaluate this prediction, we conduct experiments with
Chinese and Sumerian laws that are millennia old; stimuli that preserve in fossil-like form the legal thinking of ancient law-
makers. We show that laypeople’s justice intuitions closely match the logic and content of those archaic laws. We also show
covariation across different types of justice intuitions: interpersonal devaluation of offenders, judgements of moral wrongness,
mock-legislated punishments and perpetrator shame—suggesting that multiple justice intuitions may be regulated by a com-
mon social-evaluative psychology. Although alternative explanations of these findings are possible, we argue that they are
consistent with the assumption that the origin of criminal law is a cognitively sophisticated human nature.

Study description Quantitative, correlational study
Research sample We collected data with Amazon Mechanical Turk from 299 United States participants and 300 Indian participants (Experiment 1); 300
United States participants and 300 Indian participants (Experiment 2); 301 United States participants and 300 Indian participants
(Experiment 3). The samples are not nationally representative. Amazon Mechanical Turk was used to recruit participants on the grounds
that: “Our analyses of demographic characteristics suggest that MTurk participants are at least as diverse and more representative of
noncollege populations than those of typical Internet and traditional samples. Most important, we found that the quality of data
provided by MTurk met or exceeded the psychometric standards associated with published research” (Buhrmester, Kwang, & Gosling,
2011, p. 5)
Sampling strategy Participants were convenience-sampled. Standard power analyses to determine sample size could not be conducted because analyses
(correlations) are computed over the sample of actions (fixed in quantity), not over participants. However, pilot data suggested that 48
participants per condition per country would yield adequate power. This number was supplemented to compensate for probable data
exclusions. We assumed a 20% loss of data due to exclusions, and thus set data collection to 60 participants per condition per country.
Data collection Data were collected online with Qualtrics.
Timing United States data: Start of data collection: January 18, 2019. End of data collection: January 18, 2019. India data: Start of data collection:
January 18, 2019. End of data collection: January 20, 2019.
Data exclusions As per the preregistration protocols, participants were excluded from analyses if they reported having college-level education in law or if
they correctly identified the legal code. This was done in order to focus the analyses on those participants who lack explicit knowledge
about the legal codes studied herein. At the end of the experiment participants were told that the offenses they rated were drawn from
a legal code; participants were asked to “guess the legal code from which those crimes were taken.” To incentivize correct guesses, we
informed participants that correct guessers would receive a bonus in addition to their participation fee. Failure to pass an attention check
also resulted in exclusion from analysis. Thus, participants were excluded from analysis if they met one or more of the three criteria
noted above. We collected data from 299 US participants and 300 Indian participants (Experiment 1); 300 US participants and 300 Indian
participants (Experiment 2); 301 US participants and 300 Indian participants (Experiment 3). Exclusions of participants who met one or
more of the three exclusion criteria yielded usable data from: 275 US participants and 203 Indian participants (Experiment 1); 289 US
participants and 222 Indian participants (Experiment 2); 250 US participants and 207 Indian participants (Experiment 3).
Non-participation No participant declined to participate.
Randomization In each experiment and each country participants were randomly assigned to one of five experimental conditions.

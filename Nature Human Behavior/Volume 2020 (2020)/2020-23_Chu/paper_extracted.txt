Air pollution as a determinant of food delivery and
related plastic waste
Junhong Chu 1 ✉, Haoming Liu 2,3 ✉ and Alberto Salvo 4 ✉
Plastic waste is a growing environmental concern. The food delivery industry is criticized for its environmental impact, espe-
cially its current use of plastic packaging. At the same time, the environment impacts the industry. We show that air pollution
is a behavioural driver of food delivery consumption in the urban developing world. Our hypothesis is that individuals are more
likely to order delivery when their personal cost of exposure to the outdoor environment rises. We surveyed office workers in
three Chinese cities and found that an increase of 100 μg m–3 in particulate matter pollution (PM2.5) raised the propensity to
order food delivery by two-fifths of the sample mean. We used photographic evidence to quantify disposable plastic in meal
delivery. Data from an online delivery platform with a broad customer base indicate a smaller, but still substantial, causal link
between air quality and food delivery. Overall, air pollution control brings plastic waste co-benefits.

Study description Both studies can be described as "quantitative observational."
Research sample Data for study 1 were collected by a market research firm, chosen (on the basis of technical expertise and price) from over one dozen
market research firms that we contacted initially. All correspondence is available upon request. 251 office workers in three Chinese
cities (Beijing, Shenyang, and Shijiazhuang) were surveyed on their daily work decision and lunch choice, each worker over 11
consecutive workdays, during the winter and spring of 2018. Study 2 draws on the 2016 Beijing order book of an online food delivery
platform. This is an existing dataset. The raw data consist of a sample of 352,972 users and include 3.5 million completed orders with
delivery locations across Beijing.
Sampling strategy Study 1's choice of just over 80 office workers in each of three cities with high and variable particle pollution, each surveyed over 11
workdays, totaling about 2,700 observations (including days that workers did not go into the office), was judged to be a sufficient
sample based on previous experience. We were agnostic as to the size of an effect and no specific sample-size calculation was
performed. The sample size is fairly comparable to a variety of panel studies linking air pollution or other environmental variables to
health and other socioeconomic outcomes (see Methods). According to the food delivery firm, the dataset used in study 2 is
representative of the composition of its 2016 user base in Beijing. Ambient environmental conditions were collected independently
by environmental and meteorological agencies (see the captions to Supplementary Tables 2 and 10 for studies 1 and 2, respectively).
Data collection Study 1 respondents (office workers) informed their day's work decision and lunch choice via mobile devices. Each day over 11
consecutive workdays, a worker would receive a reminder on her phone with a link to a short online survey, sent at 10:30 am. To
reduce recall error, each worker-day specific link was deactivated at 10 am the next day. The data are observational - i.e., there was
no experimental condition - and the research objective (how visible ambient particle pollution determines lunch choices) was
withheld from the provider. Study 2 data were obtained from a food delivery firm's ordering and billing system.
Timing Study 1's data collection started on January 17, 2018 and ended on June 4, 2018. Study 2's data were collected, through the firm's
course of business, throughout 2016.
Data exclusions No data were excluded from the analyses.
Non-participation Study 1 participants were registered in the market research firm's panel and were chosen to be representative of office workers in
three Chinese cities (Beijing, Shenyang, and Shijiazhuang). In all, our sample consists of 251 workers, 242 of whom completed the
questions on each of the 11 days. Our objective was to collect an individual-level sample in which a link between outdoor air quality
and food delivery might be pronounced, to complement our analysis of a food delivery firm's order book - Study 2. Study 2's data
were extracted "as is" from the firm's order book.
Randomization Neither study consists of an experiment. The data are observational. There is no intervention by the researcher (the market research
firm, the food delivery platform, or us).

11 Abstract: While genetic influence on voter turnout is substantial (typically 40-50%), the underlying
12 mechanisms remain a puzzle. Across the social sciences, research suggests that “resources for
13 politics” (as indexed notably by educational attainment and intelligence test performance) constitute
14 a central cluster of factors predicting electoral participation. Educational attainment and intelligence
15 test performance are heritable. This suggests that the genotypes that enhance these phenotypes
16 could positively predict turnout. To test this, we conduct the first genome-wide complex trait
17 analysis of individual-level turnout. We utilize two samples from the Danish iPSYCH case-cohort
18 study including a nationally representative sample and a sample of individuals who are particularly
19 vulnerable to political alienation due to psychiatric conditions (n = 13,884 and 33,062). Using
20 validated individual-level turnout data from the administrative records at the polling place, genetic
21 correlations, and mendelian randomization, we show a substantial genetic overlap between turnout
22 and educational attainment and intelligence test performance, respectively.
2
23 Representative democracies are premised on the electoral participation of their citizens. Without
24 politically engaged citizens, democracy lacks its central impetus, and if some groups are
25 systematically excluded from the political process or decide to opt out, key societal problems will
26 most likely remain unaddressed, as parliaments do not represent the views and priorities of their
27 citizens. Yet, while the act of voting in this sense can be considered a fundamental public good, a
28 large literature documents strong disparities among citizens in their level of electoral participation,
29 with some not voting in elections at all (e.g., refs. 1‒4). These disparities exist both in the general
30 population and in segments that are particularly vulnerable to political exclusion and alienation
31 (e.g., those with psychiatric conditions). Why do some people devotedly vote in elections while
32 others consistently remain unengaged?
33 A large body of twin-based studies have shown that individual differences in voter turnout
34 are strongly associated with genetic variation, with heritability estimates that are typically between
35 40-50% but up to 72% (e.g., 5-9). Importantly, these findings raise the fundamental question of
36 how genes and voter turnout are linked (e.g. 5; 10). As emphasized by social scientists, “[a]t this
37 early point in the research, there remains a ‘black box’” (ref. 11: 45). Together with a call that
38 better theorizing "must occur" (12: 5), critiques have also been raised against existing twin and
39 candidate gene studies due to methodological limitations (13, 14).
40 To advance our understanding of how genes and voter turnout are linked, we follow recent
41 studies (5, 10) and integrate the genetic and the social science paradigms of the micro-level
42 foundations of voter turnout. Classic social science models of electoral participation emphasize that
43 voting imposes opportunity costs on voters in the form of time, money, effort, and cognitive
44 investment (15-17). In the face of these costs, individual differences in educational attainment and
45 intelligence test performance constitute key predictors of political participation because they index
46 “resources for politics” that reduce the costs of voting. The correlations predicted by the resource
3
47 model between political participation and educational attainment and cognitive performance,
48 respectively, have found wide empirical support (e.g. 17, 18), and can also be observed in
49 Denmark, the site of the present study (19, see also SI Appendix, S2.3 and Supplementary Table 5).
50 From a genetics perspective, however, it is important to note that educational attainment and
51 performance on intelligence tests are themselves genetically influenced and correlated traits (e.g.,
52 20-24). Hence, the genetic variation that predicts individual differences in educational attainment
53 and intelligence test performance may also predict variation in voter turnout. Integrating the genetic
54 and social science paradigms regarding the roots of voter turnout, we therefore ask: To what extent
55 is the genetic variation that underlies educational attainment and intelligence test performance
56 associated with individual differences in voter turnout? We argue and demonstrate that the
57 genotypes that predict individual differences in educational attainment and intelligence test
58 performance also predict individual differences in voter turnout.
59 For decades, scholars have especially considered education as the “universal solvent” that
60 predicts high political participation and, hence, the functioning of democracy (25: 324; 26, see also
61 4, 17, 27). Education increases “civic skills and political knowledge,” which function as
62 mechanisms underlying participation (28: 689), and these mechanisms are associated with
63 participation via “knowledge about where, when and for whom to vote” (ref. 6: 3), the ability to
64 communicate effectively, cognitive skills to understand policies, a sense of civic duty and
65 responsibility (4, 17), and the socioeconomic status that provides access to decision makers and
66 politically relevant information (29, see also 26). Educational attainment is also associated with
67 personality traits such as openness to experience, which predict individual awareness and interest in
68 politics (e.g. 30, 12, 10) and prior research has furthermore argued that a higher educational
69 achievement could also reflects an individual’s motivation and and self-control (85-86).
70 Importantly, these cognitive and non-cognitive mechanisms all reduce the individual’s voting costs
4
71 (17). The causal interpretation of these relationships remain an ongoing discussion. In the genetic
72 literature, research has found that the SNP associations that predict variation in educational
73 attainment and cognitive performance also to some extent predict personality traits (e.g. 87),
74 socioeconomic status (e.g. 88-89), and wealth (90). In line with these results there is ongoing debate
75 in the political science literature regarding the causal interpretation of the relationship between
76 phenotypic educational attainment and voter turnout and the underyling mechanisms (see 28, 31 for
77 reviews). Persson (28: 701) sums up that education might capture an individual’s cognitive skills,
78 motivation, and social network. The question of the exact causal pathways notwithstanding, “[t]he
79 relationship between education and voter turnout ranks among the most extensively documented
80 correlations” (31: 174), and educational attainment is considered one of the most important
81 predictors of political participation (e.g. 4, 17, 27, 28).
82 In addition to educational attainment, performance on intelligence tests is also studied as a
83 predictor of voter turnout (e.g., 32, 33). While intelligence test performance should be interpreted
84 with care, intelligence test performance has been viewed as an indicator of cognitive skills (see 96
85 for a discussion) and a reliable predictor of political efficacy, which is associated with higher
86 political participation (34: 711). Studies in Western countries have found that those who perform
87 well on intelligence tests in childhood are more interested in politics in adulthood and more likely
88 to participate in elections and engage in other forms of political participation (e.g., refs. 32, 33).
89 Importantly, like education, intelligence test performance has also been found to be correlated with
90 socioeconomic status (e.g., refs. 36, 37), which is also associated with higher political participation
91 (17).
92 Intelligence test performance and educational attainment are highly heritable and correlated
93 polygenic traits (r = 0.73) (38, 39). The trait-enhancing markers are spread throughout the human
g
94 genome (40; see also 41, 42; see also 43 for a review), with GCTA-GREML estimates of 51%
5
95 narrow-sense heritability for intelligence (44) and 21% for educational attainment (45). Hence,
96 integrating the social science resource model of political participation with the genetic literature on
97 the heritability of educational attainment and intelligence test performance gives rise to the key
98 correlational hypothesis that the genetic variance that predicts educational attainment and
99 intelligence test performance, respectively, predict individual differences in electoral participation.
100 In testing this hypothesis, we also seek to overcome methodological shortcomings in the
101 existing literature. Prior studies investigating the heritability of voter turnout have relatively low
102 external validity due to an almost exclusive reliance on samples of twins (n = 396‒ 3616) (see refs.
103 5-10). One molecular genetics study relied on a candidate gene approach conducted on a random
104 American sample (46) but concerns have been raised about its replicability (14). A recent study
105 performed the first genome-wide association analysis for voting behavior but used regional data on
106 voting behavior and did not analyze individual level-turnout (91). All but three studies have utilized
107 data from Americans, which further limits the external validity. Interestingly, one of these studies
108 with a Swedish sample produced mixed findings regarding the heritability of voting (5).
109 Furthermore, it is important to note that all research efforts have been directed towards
110 investigating the heritability of voting (e.g., refs. 5-9, 46) and the underlying mechanisms [e.g. 5) in
111 the general population. To the best of our knowledge, no study has directly investigated the genetics
112 of individual-level turnout in vulnerable subpopulations where political non-participation is a more
113 wide-spread phenomenon than in the general population (see SI Appendix, Supplementary Table 1
114 and Supplementary Figure 1, see also e.g., 56, 60).
115 Prior studies addressing the heritability of voter turnout also face issues regarding internal
116 validity. Most past studies have utilized the classic reared-together-twin design, which typically
117 confounds the potential influence of genes and shared environment (see e.g. 47 for a discussion).
118 Given these limitations to the internal validity, scholars have warned that twin studies “can neither
6
119 prove nor refute the argument for a genetic basis of political traits such as (…) voting turnout” (ref.
120 48: 350). Finally, most studies rely on self-reported voter turnout, which is known to be associated
121 with misreporting and over-reporting and, hence limits measurement validity (e.g., 49).
122 To address these limitations to external, internal, and measurement validity, we conduct a
123 comprehensive investigation of the link between genetics and electoral participation. To increase
124 internal and external validity, we move beyond the classic twin design and rely on a dataset of
125 comprehensively genotyped individuals from the Danish population-based iPSYCH Case-Cohort
126 sample (PMID: 28924187). It includes a representative sample of the entire population born 1981‒
127 2005 in Denmark and a psychiatric sample of persons diagnosed with anorexia, schizophrenia,
128 mood disorders, bipolar affective disorder, autism, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder
129 (ADHD) born in Denmark in the same period (see ref. 50 and SI Appendix, S1 for supplemental
130 details).
131 As the overall setting, the Danish electoral context is substantially more inclusive than the
132 United States where most past studies of the heritability of turnout have been conducted, and where
133 e.g. comprehensive voter identification laws shape turnout (51). In Denmark, all eligible citizens
134 are automatically registered to vote, and much is done to enable disabled people and others on the
135 margins of society to vote in elections. Hence, the barriers to electoral participation are relatively
136 low in Denmark.
137 Individuals with a psychiatric condition are an important subpopulation to study: According
138 to WHO, one in four people in the world become affected by a psychiatric or neurological condition
139 during their lives (52). Individuals with a psychiatric condition are potentially vulnerable to social
140 and political exclusion (e.g., 53-58, see also ref. 59). First, we might expect that psychiatric
141 condition, on average, will lower turnout. Using the logic of the resource model, prior research thus
142 argues that poor health may “hinder the acquisition of other resources such as civic skills, time and
7
143 money” (58: 475). Second, we might expect that individuals with certain psychiatric conditions are
144 particularly vulnerable to exclusion. For example, prior studies have argued that impeded cognitive
145 functioning, which is central in psychiatric diagnoses such as ADHD, “negatively influence
146 individuals’ ability to process information related to elections” (60: 167). Having a psychiatric
147 condition may also lead people to actively deprioritize voting as too costly in terms of time, effort
148 and attention and therefore voluntarily opt out of the political process. Finally, other studies argue
149 that some types of mental conditions such as depression can reduce “the somatic capacity of an
150 individual and therefore reduce the resources an individual has for political participation” (56:
151 1229). If individuals with psychiatric conditions don’t vote in elections, parliaments may not
152 represent the views and priorities of this group of citizens, and this augments the risk that key
153 problems and needs relevant to them will remain underprioritized.
154 In our psychiatric sample, individuals with a psychiatric diagnosis were on average 15‒32%
155 less likely to vote in the three elections under study than the general Danish population (see SI
156 Appendix, S2, Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figure 1). Individuals diagnosed with
157 anorexia have the highest turnout frequency and are descriptively indistinguishable from the
158 representative sample. Individuals diagnosed with ADHD have the lowest turnout and are 28‒54%
159 less likely to vote than the average Danish population (see SI Appendix, S2, Supplementary Table 1
160 and Supplementary Figure 1). Studying the link between genes and voter turnout in both a
161 nationally representative sample and a sub-population of individuals with a psychiatric diagnosis
162 who are vulnerable to political exclusion increases the external validity of the results.
163 To maximize measurement validity, we move beyond self-reports of turnout and integrate
164 the genetic data with population-based turnout obtained directly from the administrative records at
165 the polling place of whether Danish citizens voted in the 2015 national, 2014 European, and 2013
166 municipal elections (see SI Appendix, S1.2 for supplemental details). The saliency of the election
8
167 differs substantially across these types of elections, most simply apparent from the general turnout
168 in them. The national election constitutes a high-saliency election with a turnout of 86% in 2015.
169 The municipal election in 2013 represents a medium-high-saliency election with a turnout of 72%.
170 Finally, European elections generally have low saliency in Denmark, and turnout in 2014 was only
171 56%. Investigating our prediction across three types of elections further increases external validity.
172 With 46,946 unrelated individuals, this provides a dataset that excels in terms of: 1. sample
173 size; 2. measurement validity as electoral participation is objective rather than self-reported; 3.
174 internal validity as the research design moves beyond the limitations of the classic reared-together
175 twin design; and 4. external validity as the dataset includes a random, nationally representative
176 sample of more than 13,000 individuals and one of the world’s largest samples of a sub-population
177 of individuals with a psychiatric diagnosis, and covers three types of elections.

221 higher in the low-saliency European parliamentary election than in the national election (p = 0.044,

227 Tingsten’s (66) law of dispersion, which predicts that the participatory gap between high- and low-

232 sample compared to previous studies, given the general inclusiveness of the Danish electoral
233 context. Regarding the psychiatric sample, it should also be noted that we find no statistically
234 significant difference between SNP heritability of turnout in the European election of 2014 and in
235 the national election of 2015 (p = 0.4099, z = 0.2277) or the municipal election of 2013 (p =
236 0.2561, z = 0.6551) (see Analyses of differences in heritability across elections” in the methods

240 Predictive Utility of Polygenic Scores
241 Next, we investigate whether the genotypes that predict individual differences in educational
242 attainment and performance on intelligence tests also predict individual differences in voter turnout.
243 We use polygenic scores (PS) to measure genetic correlates of educational attainment and
244 performance on intelligence tests (cf. ref. 38, 68-69). A PS is an index that aggregates the effects of
245 all the DNA-variants associated with a given trait to predict an outcome (e.g. 24). Specifically, a PS
246 is the sum of the products of additive counts of SNP-alleles (e.g., 0, 1, or 2 copies of the rarer of
247 two alleles at a given variant) and the corresponding per-allele effect sizes estimated from a
248 genome-wide association study for a given trait. PSs have been shown to predict educational
249 attainment (ref. 68) and intelligence test performance (38) reliably. Yet, the predictive ability of
250 polygenic scores should be interpreted knowing that the proportion of explained variation is
251 typically low (ref. 70: 2). About 11‒13% of the variance in education and 7-10% of the variance in
252 cognitive performance can be predicted from the PS for educational attainment, and 5.2% of the
253 variation in intelligence test performance can be predicted from the PS for intelligence test
254 performance (38, 68).
255 Fig. 2 shows voter turnout within each percentile of the distribution for the PS for
256 educational attainment and performance on intelligence tests, respectively, across the three
257 elections in the psychiatric and nationally representative samples. The results show that higher PS
258 for educational attainment and intelligence test performance, respectively, correspond with
259 increased voter turnout across all three elections in both samples. The variance in voter turnout
260 explained by polygenic scores for educational attainment and intelligence test performance for each
261 election across both the nationally representative and psychiatric samples and the associated p-
262 values are detailed in the SI Appendix, S5.2, Supplementary Table 7. These results indicate that the
12
263 relationships between voter turnout and PS for educational attainment and intelligence test
264 performance, respectively, are highly statistically significant (p < 1.61e-7 or lower) and that the
265 gain in Nagelkerke pseudo r2 ranges from 0.0041 (95% CI: 0.0016 – 0.0078) to 0.0317 (95% CI:
266 0.0235 – 0.0413) when one of the PSs was added to a baseline logistic model which had the first ten
267 principal components of genetic ancestry as explanatory variables (see SI Appendix S5.2,
268 Supplementary Table 7). While voter turnout is overall higher in the nationally representative
269 sample, the predictive value of the polygenic scores retains statistical significance (Fig. 2 and
270 Supplementary Table 7). The consistency in predictive performance of the polygenic scores in the
271 two samples suggests that the overlap among SNPs associated with voter turnout and educational
272 attainment and intelligence test performance, respectively, is comparable in the general population
273 and in the sub-population of individuals with a psychiatric condition.
274 In interpreting the phenotypic processes producing these findings, it is relevant to consider
275 prior studies showing that SNPs associated with educational attainment may in part exert their
276 influence via personality traits (71). Social science studies have also found evidence of a
277 relationship between the big five personality traits and political participation (e.g. 12, 30, 72-74, see
278 also 75 for a review). In particular, some studies suggest that personality factors can account for
279 part of the correlation between genetics and political participation (5, see also 10: 8) and highlight
280 extraversion as a factor that “may possibly mediate the relationship between genes and political
281 predispositions known to be strongly related to political participation” (5: 291).
282 While we believe it is likely that phenotypic personality constitutes part of the causal
283 pathway from genetics to turnout, we cannot test whether the correlation between PS for
284 educational attainment and turnout is mediated by phenotypic big five personality traits or
285 phenotypic education as these variables are not available in our data. Supplemental analyses
286 reported in the SI Appendix, S5.2, Supplementary Tables S7 and S8 show, however, that the PSs
13
287 for educational attainment and intelligence test performance, respectively, predict individual
288 differences in turnout more consistently than the polygenic scores for the big five personality traits:
289 The polygenic scores for the big five personality traits display mixed predictive utility for electoral
290 turnout where the gain in Nagelkerke pseudo r2 ranges from 2e-10 to 0.0014 when the PS was added
291 to a baseline logistic regression model which had the first ten principal components of genetic
292 ancestry as explanatory variables, and p-values for the statistical significance of the PSs for the big
293 five personality traits range from 0.9754 to 4.32e-5, see SI Appendix, S5.2, Supplementary Table 8).
294 For example, across the three elections and our two samples, only in the 2014 European election in
295 the psychiatric sample do we find that the PS for extraversion significantly accounts for variation in
296 turnout. PS for openness to experience also has mixed predictive utility and no significant genetic
297 correlations with conscientiousness are observed. Only agreeableness is consistently significantly
298 associated with turnout across the three elections in the two samples. It should be noted that these
299 results could reflect the lower sample size of the dataset used to generate the genome wide
300 summary statistics for calculating the PSs for the big five personality traits. Still, these findings
301 support the more consistent predictive utility of PS for educational attainment and intelligence test
302 performance, respectively, compared to the PSs for the big five personality traits.

306 Genetic Correlation Analysis
307 Next, we explore the genetic correlation between voter turnout and traits related to educational
308 attainment and intelligence test performance. Furthermore, we explored genetic correlations
309 between voter turnout and socioeconomic factors as well as health indicators, as LDSC as
310 implemented in LD_HUB makes it possible to assess genetic correlation with many traits using
14
311 online tools (39). Specifically, we used LDHub to test the correlation between 72 traits and voter
312 turnout. Separating the correlations by election and sample, Fig. 3 shows the genetic correlations
313 between voter turnout and non-voting traits for all traits with at least one significant correlation
314 across elections and sample after FDR adjustment (the full list of genetic correlations and all
315 estimates is found in SI Appendix, S11).
316 In the psychiatric sample, we find positive genetic correlations for electoral turnout across
317 all three elections with indicators of educational attainment (i.e., college completion and years of
318 schooling) and intelligence test performance (i.e., childhood IQ). As expected, the correlations in
319 the nationally representative sample follow the same pattern, albeit with higher p-values and larger
320 error bars owing most likely to the smaller sample size (see the SI Appendix, S7.1 for analyses of
321 robustness). None of the genetic correlations in Fig. 3 show any discernible difference between the
322 two samples for any of the elections (see the SI Appendix, S7.2, Supplementary Figure 7).
323 Interestingly, negative socioeconomic factors such as number of children, younger age at
324 first childbirth, and younger parental age at death also show genetic correlations with voter turnout
325 (Fig. 3). This suggests that in addition to the PSs for education and intelligence test performance a
326 broad set of socioeconomic resources could connect genetics to electoral participation.
327 Furthermore, Fig. 3 shows a negative correlation between voter turnout and adverse physical and
328 mental health outcomes (e.g., depressive symptoms, neuroticism, ADHD). The genetic correlations
329 with depressive symptoms and ADHD symptoms underline the importance of these psychiatric
330 conditions as predisposing factors for non-voting. Even among individuals where the additive sum
331 of conferred risk does not cross the threshold to manifest as a psychiatric diagnosis, instances of
332 significant correlation with voter turnout are identified for each of these traits.

336 Summary Statistics Based Mendelian Randomization analysis
337 As the final test of our prediction, we perform a mendelian randomization-based analysis (77) of
338 whether the genetic variance that predicts educational attainment and intelligence test performance,
339 respectively, predicts voter turnout. Specifically, we use markers surpassing genome-wide
340 significance (p ≤ 5x10-8) in association studies of educational attainment and intelligence test
341 performance as instrumental variables. These genetic markers for educational attainment and
342 intelligence test performance are treated as exposures and voter turnout in each election as an
343 outcome.
344 The results (see SI Appendix, S8.2, Supplementary Table 12 and Supplementary Figure 8)
345 show that an individual in the nationally representative sample who has a genetic disposition to
346 obtain education one standard deviation higher than the population mean was predicted to be 2.66
347 (SE = 0.1064, p = 3.76 ×10-20) times more likely to vote in the municipal election in 2013, 3.14 (SE
348 = 0.129, p = 7.51x10-19) times more likely to vote in the European Parliament election in 2014, and
349 3.32 (SE = 0.1439, p = 6.66x10-17) times more likely to vote in the national election in 2015. An
350 individual in the psychiatric sample with a genetic disposition to obtain education one standard
351 deviation higher than the mean in the psychiatric sample was predicted to be 2.97 (SE = 0.0866, p =
352 3.44x10-36) times more likely to vote in the 2014 European election, 2. 65 (SE = 0.08, p = 3.72x10-
353 34) times more likely to vote in the 2015 national election, and 2. 67 (SE = 0.0656, p = 1.26x10-50)
354 times more likely to vote in the 2013 municipal election.

356 the effect size is smaller. An individual in the nationally representative sample with a genetic
357 disposition to perform on intelligence tests one standard deviation higher than the mean in the
358 general population was predicted to be 1.85 (SE = 0.142, p = 1.47x10-5) times more likely to vote in
16
359 the national election in 2015, 2.15 (SE = 0.1277, p = 1.87x10-9) times more likely to vote in the
360 European Parliament election in 2014 and 1.77 times (SE = 0.1051, p = 4.6x10-8) more likely to

362 genetic disposition to perform on intelligence tests one standard deviation higher than the mean of
363 this sample was predicted to be 1.78 (SE = 0.078902. p = 2.58x10-13) times more likely to vote in

367 As a negative control, we repeat the analysis above using markers that surpass the genome-
368 wide significance threshold for height obtained using summary statistics from the GIANT
369 consortium as instrumental variables. The GSMR analysis of height did not suggest any significant
370 relationship between genetic dispositions towards height and turnout in any election across either of
371 the samples (see SI Appendix, S8.2, Supplementary Table 12).
372 Still, as our data are observational and Mendelian randomization analysis requires strong
373 identifying assumptions and does not account for any environmental influences on the exposures of
374 interest towards the outcome the results should be seen as correlation evidence that PS for
375 educational attainment and intelligence test performance, respectively, predicts voter turnout (see
376 SI Appendix, S9 for supplemental discussion of our analysis and the assumptions for mendelian

379 Conclusion
380 It is essential to the functioning and legitimacy of democracy that citizens vote in elections.
381 Nonetheless, large inequalities in voter turnout exist. While a decade of twin-based studies has
382 shown that variability in voter turnout is shaped by genetics (e.g., refs. 5, 7-9, 13), the fundamental
17
383 question of the mechanisms underlying this connection largely remains a “black box” (12: 89).
384 Traditionally, social science models of voter turnout have “typically ignored genetic or biological
385 factors” (ref. 78: 912).
386 In this article, we follow recent studies (5, 10) and seek to advance our understanding of
387 how genes and voter turnout are linked by integrating the genetic and the social science paradigms
388 of the micro-level foundations of voter turnout, arguing that the genetic variances that predict
389 educational attainment and intelligence tests performance predict individual differences in voter
390 turnout. To test this argument, we have conducted the first genome-wide complex trait analysis of
391 the heritability of individual-level electoral participation using a nationally representative sample
392 and a population sample of individuals diagnosed with anorexia, schizophrenia, mood disorders,
393 bipolar affective disorder, autism, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Our
394 methodological triangulation combining polygenic scores, genetic correlations, and mendelian
395 randomization analyses consistently supports that the genetic variants that predict educational
396 attainment and intelligence test performance also predict voter turnout.
397 While our findings provide evidence for the predicted genetic associations, our results are
398 mute on the causal interpretation. First, as we work with observational data and Mendelian
399 randomization analysis requires strong identifying assumption our results are correlational. We
400 cannot determine whether the observed genetic correlations reflect causal effects of phenotypic
401 education and phenotypic intelligence test performance on voter turnout. For example, some
402 research suggests that education works at least in part as a proxy for pre-adult factors such as family
403 socio-economic status (e.g. see 28 for a review, see also 79), and recent research has found
404 evidence of
405 genetic nurturing where genes influence educational attainment indirectly via family environment
406 (79).
18
407 Second, the resources for politics associated with, for example, educational achievement
408 are multifaceted and include particular personality traits as well as material resources. Thus, we find
409 it likely that part of the relationship between genetic dispositions toward educational achievement
410 and intelligence test performance, respectively, could be mediated by phenotypic personality-
411 related resources. Furthermore, as the PS for educational attainment is also predictive of
412 performance on cognitive tests (68) part of the significant relationship between the PS for
413 educational attainment and turnout could be explained by the broader set of factors underlying such
414 performance. While our results are consistent with the argument that educational attainment and
415 intelligence test performance, respectively, represent a link between genes and voter turnout they
416 cannot provide conclusive evidence in support of such a causal interpretation.
417 The above limitations notwithstanding, our study contributes by moving beyond the
418 limitations of the reared-together-twin design to analyze the heritability of individual-level voter
419 turnout using data on single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Given the strong critiques raised in
420 the social sciences of existing twin-based studies of the heritability of turnout (e.g. 13, 14), this is
421 important as methodological triangulation helps overcome some of the limitations of twin-based
422 studies and increases internal validity. Considering the typical differences in the size of h2
SNP
423 estimates and twin study estimates (80), the h2 estimates in the psychiatric sample are
SNP
424 proportionally consistent with past (American) twin study estimates. Interestingly, we did not
425 observe statistically significant h2 estimates in the nationally representative sample in the high
SNP
426 and medium-high-saliency elections in 2013 and 2015, respectively, but only in the low-saliency
427 election for the European election in 2014. Yet, a) the overlapping confidence intervals with the
428 psychiatric sample, b) the power analysis, and c) the heritability estimates from the pedigree-
429 defined familial relationships suggests that the non-significant h2 estimates for the high and
SNP
19
430 medium-high elections in the nationally representative sample in part reflect lower statistical power
431 in this sample.
432 Importantly, however, the differences in heritability between elections and samples are
433 likely to also reflect substantive contextual differences. First, in the nationally representative
434 sample, we find higher heritability of turnout in the low-saliency European election compared to the
435 high saliency national election; second, our results from the nationally representative sample
436 suggest that the heritability for national and highly salient elections possibly could be lower in
437 general Danish population sample compared to prior estimates from United States; These findings
438 are consistent with Tingsten’s (66) law of dispersion, which stipulates that individual differences in
439 resources for politics have stronger influence on whether people vote in elections with greater
440 participatory costs as reflected in, for example, lower general turnout. It should be noted that these
441 results are based on data from a single country – Denmark. Our results support the value of
442 additional investigations of differences in the heritability of turnout between elections with different
443 costs of participation in different types of electoral systems to further explore the replicability and
444 external validity of this finding.
445 Another venue for further research is to study whether heritability of turnout varies across
446 psychiatric conditions. Our analysis focuses on average patterns for individuals with a psychiatric
447 condition. Still certain types of psychiatric conditions may generate particularly high costs of
448 voting. Drawing on the logic of Tingsten’s law of dispersion (66), this could imply that these
449 conditions increase the role of genetic dispositions.
450 Overall, the results extend understanding of the patterns underlying inequalities in voter
451 turnout. They show that the genetic variance that predicts educational attainment and intelligence
452 test performance, respectively, also predicts individual differences in voter turnout. This integration
453 of the social science and genetic paradigms of voter turnout reveals new linkages between social
20
454 and political inequalities in the general population and in the sub-populations that are particularly
455 vulnerable to political exclusion due to psychiatric diagnoses.

457 Materials and Methods
458 Data. The iPSYCH Danish case-cohort sample consists of two large samples of comprehensively
459 genotyped individuals: a nationally representative sample (n = 30,000) of individuals born in
460 Denmark 1981‒2005 and a population sample of all individuals born in the same period with one of
461 the following psychiatric diagnoses: schizophrenia, mood disorders, bipolar affective disorder,
462 autism, ADHD, and anorexia (N = 63,080) (see ref. 50 for details).
463 The iPSYCH samples were merged with population-based records of whether Danish citizens voted
464 in the 2015 national, 2014 European, and 2013 municipal elections in Denmark. To maximize
465 measurement validity, turnout data was collected directly from the voting files at the polling
466 stations (see SI Appendix, S1 for details). We excluded individuals who show second degree or
467 higher relatedness, individuals who were not from the genetically homogenous population as
468 identified by PCA analysis, and individuals who fail standard GWAS quality control tests for
469 association tests. Finally, we limit the analysis to individuals who were 18 years old or older - and
470 hence had reach the Danish voting age to become eligible to vote in a public election in 2015 (see
471 SI Appendix, S1 for details).
472 The iPSYCH has been approved by the Danish Scientific Ethics Committee, the Danish Health
473 Data Authority, the Danish Data Protection Agency, Statistics Denmark, and the Danish Neonatal
474 Screening Biobank Steering Committee (see also ref. 50: 9). The iPSYCH is “an example of Danish
475 studies based upon the combination of registers and biological material stored in biobanks” (94:
476 1574). On 28 August 2012, in accordance with the Act on Research Ethics Review of Health
21
477 Research Projects (in Danish: Komitéloven), Section 10 (1), the Central Denmark Region
478 Committees on Health Research Ethics, Committee II has granted the iPSYCH exemption from
479 obtaining informed consent from participants (https://ipsych.dk/en/data-security/health-research-
480 and-ethical-approval/). The committee’s “processing and approval of the iPSYCH project was in
481 accordance with applicable law and practice at the time of notification.” (https://ipsych.dk/en/data-
482 security/health-research-and-ethical-approval/). This waiver also applies to our study. Participants
483 for the iPSYCH project were recruited according to consent by non-opt out from a national research
484 program (see also 92 and 95). Participants ”can opt out of having any biological materiale used for
485 research without specific informed consent” (94: 1574). It is possible for participants to opt out of
486 the biobank storage at any time (95: 531, see also 95: 531 for additional description of briefing and
487 opt-out procedures, and 94 for additional discussion of the scientific ethical standards in iPSYCH).
488 The iPSYCH has data protection measures in place that comply with Danish and European
489 legislation (94:1575).

491 Measures. Voting in each election was coded as 1 = voted and 0 = did not vote. All analyses
492 include individuals eligible to vote at the time of the election and who were Danish citizens.
493 Polygenic scores (PS) for educational attainment and intelligence test performance were
494 calculated using summary statistics from large recent studies of associations between genetic
495 markers and educational attainment in 766,345 individuals (excluding 23andme cohort) (68) and
496 intelligence in 269,867 individuals of primarily European ethnicity (38) (See SI Appendix, S5.1 for
497 details on the base datasets for the polygenic scores and the target dataset).
498 Prior research indicates that the predictive ability of educational attainment varies across
499 samples (68). Only limited available data exist to speak to the accuracy of PS for educational
500 attainment in Danish samples, but prior research suggests that PS for educational attainment
22
501 predicted about 15% of the variance in educational attainment in a small sample of 1,459 Danish
502 individuals (68). In terms of predictive accuracy, this is comparable to the size of the general
503 estimate of about 11-13%.
504 Polygenic scores (PSs) for the big five personality traits were calculated using summary
505 statistics from large recent studies of associations between genetic markers and the big five
506 personality traits (81) using summary statistics from 175,375 adult individuals, obtained from the
507 genetics of personality consortium (http://www.tweelingenregister.org/GPC/) as described in (81).
508 To construct the PSs, we used PRSice v.1.25 (https://choishingwan.github.io/PRSice/) with
509 default parameters (clump-r2 = 0.1, clump-kb = 250, clump-p = 1) to sum the products of the effect
510 size of each individual marker with the associated additive genotype for every individual in the
511 iPSYCH determined to be of European origin using principal component analysis. See SI
512 Appendix, S2, Supplementary Tables S2-4 for summary statistics for all measures and demographic
513 sample characteristics.

515 Statistical analysis. All reported tests of statistical significance are two-tailed.
516 Following standard practices, all analyses focus on genetically homogenous (i.e., broadly
517 northern European) ancestry respondents (cf. ref. 69). Quality control, haplotype estimation and
518 genotype imputation protocols undertaken on the iPSYCH dataset are described in detail in SI
519 Appendix, S1.1.2. Briefly, the iPSYCH cohort of 78,050 individuals genotyped at 554,360 genomic
520 loci in 23 waves. After an initial QC based on principal component analysis using common (minor
521 allele frequency > 5%) high quality markers common to the iPSYCH and the full 1000 genomes
522 phase 3 variant calls, followed by principal component analysis and outlier detection, 75,501
523 samples of a homogenous genetic origin were selected for SNP level QC. After excluding rare
524 markers (minor allele frequency < 1%), those failing tests for Hardy Weinberg Equilibrium within
23
525 controls and markers with high missingness, a total of 246,539 SNPs were phased using SHAPEIT3
526 and imputed to 80,707,375 SNPs in 10 batches using IMPUTE2 and the full set of 1000 genomes
527 phase 3 haplotypes as the reference. Following stringent post imputation QC to exclude SNPs with
528 low imputation INFO scores, rare SNPs (minor allele frequency < 0.001), SNPs with high
529 missingness, differential missingness between cases and controls, SNPs showing significant
530 association to a genotyping wave or imputation batch, 11,601,089 SNPs were retained. Sample
531 level QC involved excluding samples with greater than second degree relatedness, samples showing
532 discordance from documented gender, duplicate samples, samples with abnormal heterozygosity
533 not explained by admixture, samples with high missingness and samples which deviate from a
534 homogenous genetic background as identified by ancestral birth records in conjunction with
535 principal component analysis. This resulted in a total of 65,535 samples which were used for all
536 complex trait analyses. All reported statistical tests were two-sided, except where explicitly
537 indicated.

574 Nagelkerke pseudo r2. The variance explained in election turnout was calculated as the gain
575 in pseudo r2 after adding polygenic score as the explanatory variable to a baseline logistic

584 Mendelian randomization analyses. We used the GCTA (63) with the summary statistics
585 from the GWAS of voter turnout and summary statistics from the latest genome-wide association
586 studies for educational attainment, intelligence test performance, and height available in the public
587 domain. Individuals of European origin in the 1000 genome phase 3 dataset were utilized to supply
588 linkage disequilibrium information between the SNPs. We conducted HEIDI outlier heterogeneity
589 tests (see the SI Appendix, S8 for supplementary information about the Mendelian randomization
590 analysis and robustness checks).
591 One of the methodological assumptions with summary statistics based GSMR analysis is
592 that there is no sample overlap between the genome wide association study samples utilized for the
593 exposure and outcome of interest. We have made sure that the iPSYCH samples used to perform
594 genome wide association studies of voter turnout were not used in meta-analysis of any of the
595 exposure traits.
26
596 Another assumption of GSMR is that the effect of the exposure on the outcome is linear
597 which might not always be true. While we use the HEIDI test to detect and exclude pleiotropic
598 SNPs, the power to detect pleiotropy relies on the sample size of the GWAS from which summary
599 statistics are obtained and the effect sizes of the pleiotropic SNPs (84). The large disparity in the
600 sample sizes of the exposure and outcome traits restricts the elimination of all pleiotropic SNPs in
601 our analysis.

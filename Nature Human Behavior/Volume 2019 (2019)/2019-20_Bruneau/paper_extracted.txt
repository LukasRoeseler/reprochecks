A collective blame hypocrisy intervention
enduringly reduces hostility towards Muslims
Emile G. Bruneau 1,2*, Nour S. Kteily 3 and Ana Urbiola 4
Hostility towards outgroups contributes to costly intergroup conflict. Here we test an intervention to reduce hostility towards
Muslims, a frequently targeted outgroup. Our ‘collective blame hypocrisy’ intervention highlights the hypocrisy involved in the
tendency for people to collectively blame outgroup but not ingroup members for blameworthy actions of individual group mem-
bers. Using both within-subject and between-subject comparisons in a preregistered longitudinal study in Spain, we find that our
intervention reduces collective blame of Muslims and downstream anti-Muslim sentiments relative to a matched control condition
and that the effects of the intervention persist one month and also one year later. We replicate the benefits of the intervention in
a second study. The effects are mediated by reductions in collective blame and moderated by individual differences in preference
for consistency. Together, these data illustrate that the collective blame hypocrisy intervention enduringly reduces harmful inter-
group attitudes associated with conflict escalation, particularly among those who value consistency in themselves and others.

Discussion
Here we demonstrate that a brief intervention making people aware
50 of potential hypocrisy in group-based collective blame significantly
reduces collective blame of Muslims and anti-Muslim sentiments.
This change in hostility towards Muslims endured over the course
of a year, despite the fact that there was a major attack by Muslim
extremists in Spain during this time. In the aftermath of the attacks
25 (that is, at T2), we found that control participants’ anti-Muslim
sentiments were significantly higher than at their T1 baseline.
Evidencing the enduring effectiveness of the intervention (see ref. 20),
participants randomly assigned to the collective blame hypocrisy
intervention one month earlier (that is, those in treatment condi-
0 tion A)—who showed significant reductions in collective blame and
anti-Muslim sentiments relative to controls at the time the interven-
High PFC Low PFC tion was administered—continued to show significantly lower levels
of these constructs after the attacks. Moreover, our analyses showed
that participants in treatment condition B—who provided base-
line measurements at T1 and then completed the intervention at
T2—were significantly less likely than control participants to show
an anti-Muslim shift from T1 to T2. Therefore, it seems that the
intervention buffered participants from the negative effects of the
attack. In a second study, we replicated the effectiveness of the inter-
vention and provided further evidence suggesting (as in study 1)
reduced collective blame as a mediator of its effects. Finally, we
showed that the intervention was most effective in reducing collec-
tive blame from its baseline levels among those who most strongly
value consistency in themselves and others.

Study description Studies 1 and 2 are quantitative studies.
Research sample Study 1: Our research sample was 643 adult Spaniards who completed our online survey and passed our attention checks and met our
inclusion criteria. Mean age = 44.79, SD = 14.80. 52.6% female. Study 2: Our research sample was 598 adult Spaniards who completed
our online survey and passed our attention checks and met our inclusion criteria. Mean age = 46.33, SD = 16.29. 45.0% female.
Sampling strategy We used an online convenience sample for both Study 1 and Study 2. The sample size in Study 1 was determined based on a power
analysis using G*Power 3.1 assuming a small to medium effect size (d=.35) with an alpha of .05 and a power of .95. No statistical
methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes, but we sought a sample size larger (per experimental condition) than in Study 1.
Data collection We used Qualtrics.com’s survey platform to record participants’ responses to our online survey. We were blind to experimental
condition during data collection.
Timing Study 1: 4th August 2017-16 September 2018; Study 2: 1st October 2018 - 4th October 2018
Data exclusions As pre-registered, participants were excluded if they were Muslim or if they failed an embedded attention check. In Study 1, a total of 5
Muslims were excluded. 61 participants failed our attention check at T1, 26 failed at T2, and 14 failed at T3. In Study 2, a total of 1
Muslim and 3 other participants who did not specify their religion were excluded. 51 participants were excluded for failing our attention
check.
Non-participation We are not aware of participants dropping out or declining participation in our studies.
Randomization Participants in both Study 1 and 2 were randomly assigned to experimental condition.

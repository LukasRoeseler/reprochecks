Questioning white losses and anti-white
discrimination in the United States
Megan Earle and Gordon Hodson
Political polarization and far-right movements across the West are thought to be partly driven by beliefs that white people
face discrimination in societies that supposedly favour non-white people. We compared perceptions of racial discrimination
with reported discrimination experiences in large, US national samples to shed light on the veracity of such beliefs. Regarding
experiences, we find that white people consistently experienced less discrimination than black people, and that declines in anti-
black discrimination have not coincided with increases in anti-white discrimination. Regarding perceptions, respondents overall
did not express zero-sum discrimination beliefs. Moreover, black respondents and Democrats perceived that black people face
much more discrimination than white people, whereas white respondents and Republicans perceived a smaller discrimination
gap between black and white people, relative to reported discrimination experiences. Overall, improvements for black people
do not seem to coincide with disadvantages for white people, and discrimination perceptions differ from reported discrimina-
tion experiences. Implications for racial attitudes, political polarization and voting behaviour are discussed.

Study description Sample 1, Sample 2, and Sample 3 were quantitative cross-sectional designs. Sample 4 was a quantitative longitudinal design.
Research sample All data were obtained from existing datasets collected by third-parties. Sample 1 data contained a nationally representative sample of
American adults collected by ANES (N = 5955; Mage = 50.80, SD = 17.09; 54.7% female, 45.0% male), 79.6% of whom were White and
20.4% of whom were Black. Sample 2 was a national sample of American adults collected by NORC (N = 14509, Mage = 48.61, SD = 17.55,
55.8% female, 44.2% male), 83.3% of whom were White and 16.7% were Black. Sample 3 consisted of hate crime reports collected by the
FBI; data from 88,738 racially motivated hate crime victims were used in analysis, 77.05% of whom were victims of anti-Black hate crimes
and 22.95% of whom were victims of anti-White hate crimes. Sample 4 contained responses from a national sample of American results
collected by the MacArthur Foundation Research Network who collected three surveys between 1995 and 2014 (Wave 1 N = 5914, Mage
= 46.92, SD = 12.85; 52.5% female, 47.5% male, 94.4% White, 5.6% Black).
Sampling strategy All sample sizes were determined by the third-parties who collected the data.
Data collection All data collection procedures were determined by the third-parties who collected the data.
Timing Data for Sample 1 were collected in 2012 and 2016. Data for Sample 2 were collected in 2002, 2006, 2010, 2012, 2014, and 2018. Data
for Data for Sample 3 were provided for every year between 1996 and 2017. Sample 4 were collected in 1995/1996, 2004-2006, and
2013/2014.
Data exclusions No data were excluded from analyses.
Non-participation In Sample 4, 3376 participants dropped out of the study between Wave 1 and Wave 3. Reasons for drop-out are not provided by the
third-party who collected this data. The number of participants who dropped out/declined participation in the other samples are not
provided by the third parties who collected the data.
Randomization Participants were not allocated into experimental groups.

Do AI-led ReproAI Checks Agree with Human Reproducibility Checks?

A comparison of the ReproAI audits with the human-coded reproducibility outcomes in the FLoRA / FReD database

Where a human code exists, AI and human verdicts align; but most FLoRA reproduction records carry no coded outcome in the current flora.csv.

Abstract

We asked whether the AI-led ReproAI reproducibility audits agree with human-coded reproducibility checks for the same studies, using the FLoRA / FReD database (forrtproject/FReD-data) as the human benchmark. We matched each of the 17 studies audited by ReproAI to its FLoRA record by DOI and used the human outcome field, which codes reproducibility (robustness is out of scope here).

FLoRA contains a record for 15 of the 17 audited studies, but only 4 carry a coded human reproducibility outcome (successful, failed, or mixed) that can be compared; the remaining records are reproductions whose outcome is NA in this snapshot. On the four comparable studies the AI and human verdicts are aligned in three cases (Goeree & Yariv: reproduced/successful; Baillon et al.: consistent/successful; Pennycook et al.: partial/mixed). In the fourth (Altmann et al.), the AI audit was unverifiable because no data were shared, whereas the human SCORE replication independently failed — showing where a data-rich human replication can settle a question that a no-data AI meta-check cannot.

Introduction

ReproAI (engine anomalyco/opencode) performs AI-led reproducibility audits of published studies, inventorying claims, re-implementing headline analyses where data and code are shared, and checking internal consistency. A natural validation question is whether such automated audits reach the same conclusions as human reproducibility checks that have already been conducted and coded by the research community.

The FLoRA database (Forrtproject’s FReD project) catalogs human reproducibility and replication efforts and codes their outcomes. This report compares the ReproAI audits with the corresponding human checks for numerical reproducibility (the outcome field); robustness is deliberately excluded.

Method

Data source

The public CSV flora.csv (forrtproject/FReD-data, main/output/flora.csv) was downloaded. For each row it pairs an original study with either a replication (a second new-data study attempting to reproduce it) or a reproduction (a re-analysis using the authors’ data/code), and records a human-coded outcome for reproducibility.

Matching

Each of the 17 ReproAI-audited studies was matched to FLoRA by DOI extracted from the original-study APA reference (apa_ref_o), with title-based cross-checks. Where a study had multiple FLoRA rows, all relevant rows (replication and reproduction) were retained.

Outcome mapping

Only the reproducibility part of the human code was used (successful / failed / mixed for replicate-type codes; the reproducibility component of any combined code). Robustness components were ignored. ReproAI grades were mapped to a comparable scale: fully reproduced / reproduced-or-consistent / partial / not-reproducible.

Results

Coverage

Of the 17 audited studies, 15 had a matching FLoRA record. 11 of these are reproduction records whose outcome is NA in the current flora.csv — i.e., a human reproduction exists but carries no comparable coded verdict in this snapshot. One study (Cordova & Kras, 2021) and the methods-only JMLR paper had no FLoRA entry. Only 4 studies have a coded human reproducibility outcome available for direct comparison.

On the three studies for which both methods produced a verdict (Goeree, Baillon, Pennycook), the AI and human conclusions agree. The fourth case (Altmann) is the most informative: the ReproAI audit was bounded by the absence of shared data and returned “not independently recomputable,” while a human SCORE new-data replication was able to test the original effect and found it did not replicate (failed). This illustrates a boundary of AI-led auditing: without data, an AI meta-check can flag unverifiability but cannot itself detect failure, which a data-collecting human replication can.

Discussion

Where a human-coded FLoRA outcome exists and a ReproAI audit produced a verdict, the two agree. The most consistent pattern is that both human and AI checks vindicate studies that share data and code, and flag studies where the underlying materials are missing or where the reported numbers do not reproduce.

The comparison is, however, limited by the FLoRA snapshot: the majority of the audited studies (11) have only a reproduction record with an outcome of NA, so no human reproducible code is currently available to benchmark them against. This is itself a finding: the set of studies directly comparable to the AI audits is small.

Limitations

  • Only the outcome field was used (reproducibility); robustness was excluded per the task.
  • Many FLoRA reproduction records carry outcome = NA in this flora.csv snapshot, limiting direct comparison to four studies.
  • Study matching relied on DOI/title; ambiguous or multi-article authors (e.g., Pennycook) required manual review.
  • A human replication tests the original effect with new data; a ReproAI audit re-derives numbers from shared materials. The two answer related but not identical questions, so “agreement” must be interpreted accordingly.

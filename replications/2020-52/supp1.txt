Supplementary Results 1 
Multilevel Models Analyses Concerning the Effect of Trial Similarity on Response Latencies 

In the main manuscript, we examined the effect of trial type by comparing dissimilar-
accurate trials, similar-accurate trials, and inaccurate trials (collapsing across dissimilar and similar 
trials) on response latencies. We elected to do so because: (a) there were few inaccurate trials on 
average (Experiment 1: 2 trials; Experiment 2: 3 trials), especially among dissimilar trials 
(Experiment 1: 1 trial; Experiment 2: 2 trials); and (b) we elected to conduct repeated measures 
Analysis of Variance (ANOVAs) for the main analyses which utilizes a listwise elimination 
approach when observations are missing on one of the repeated measures variables. Collapsing 
across inaccurate trials reduced the potential number of participants eliminated from analysis 
because of missing dissimilar inaccurate trials (Experiment 1: 25 participants; Experiment 2: 17 
participants did not have any inaccurate dissimilar trials). However, to offer a more comprehensive 
and detailed view of our results, we conducted post-hoc analyses using multilevel models. We first 
conducted a model on inaccurate trials to establish whether there was a difference between similar 
and dissimilar inaccurate trials; we then conducted a mixed level model on all trial types (dissimilar-
accurate, similar-accurate, and inaccurate) to replicate the results obtained with the ANOVAs 
reported in the main manuscript. Finally, to assess fit of our models, we conducted a chi-squared 
difference test, comparing our model to an intercept only (baseline) model.   

Effect of Similarity on Inaccurate Trials. In order to test whether there was a significant 

difference between dissimilar-inaccurate and similar-inaccurate trials we conducted a t-test and 
verified the results with a multilevel model with a fixed effect of trial similarity (dissimilar-
inaccurate, similar-inaccurate) and a random effect of subject.  

Experiment 1: We found no statistically significant effect of trial similarity among inaccurate 
trials (dissimilar: M = 3997.92, SD = 2308.17; similar: M = 4375.16, SD = 2566.40) with either the 
t-test (t(45) = -.70, p =.49, d = -.10, 95% CI = -916.02, 445.86) or the multilevel model (b = 307.44, 
t(202) = .73, p =.47, d = .04, 95% CI = -526.49, 1141.36).  

Experiment 2: We again found no evidence for statistically significant differences between 

inaccurate trial types (dissimilar: M = 5980.97, SD = 7482.14; similar: M = 5112.41, SD = 4364.38; 
t(47) = .77, p =.45, d = .11, 95% CI = -1537.99, 3434.151; b = -899.33, t(229) = -1.02, p =.31, d = -
.07, 95% CI = -2644.21, 845.56). 

Given that the effect of similarity is not detectable among inaccurate trials and the small 

number of these trials, we continued to collapse across inaccurate trials in both Experiment 1 and 2. 

Effect of Trial Type. To confirm the effects of trial type found with our ANOVA approach, 
we examined the differences between response latencies as a function of trial type with a multilevel 
model including a fixed effect of trial type (dissimilar-accurate, similar-accurate, and inaccurate) and 
a random effect of subject. Since the analyses above showed no difference between inaccurate trial

Supplementary Results 2 
Hierarchical Drift Diffusion Model (HDDM) 

Drift diffusion models typically require numerous trials in order to obtain reliable parameter 

estimates. In our manuscript, we did not rely on numerous trials. Thus, we examined whether models 
conducted at the individual level and models conducted across the entire sample provided 
converging patterns of results. For the sample level parameters, we utilized quantile probability plots 
to conduct a qualitative comparison between the observed results and the results predicted by the 
drift diffusion model (see Supplementary Figure 1). This comparison revealed a similar pattern of 
results in both Experiment 1 (Supplementary Figure 1a) and Experiment 2 (Supplementary Figure 
1b). Additionally, we also compared our sample level model to a baseline model using a chi-squared 
difference test, which revealed our model to be the better fit to our data.  

Another approach is to use a hierarchical drift diffusion model as implemented in the HDDM 

package1. HDDM allows for the estimation of drift parameters with a smaller number of trials 
compared to typical procedures by using a Bayesian estimation approach1. HDDM also allows 
estimation of inter-trial variability for each of the standard drift diffusion parameters, which we were 
not able to do in the models reported in the manuscript.  

We fit our HDDM model in python 3.6 on response times and accuracy data generated from 
the touchscreen task. HDDM fits a hierarchical model with group parameters for each trial condition 
(i.e., Similar and Dissimilar). We estimated standard drift diffusion parameters (i.e., drift rate, v; 
separation, a; and non-decision time, t0) keeping z0 fixed to 0.5 in 3 different models.  The three 
models were:  

1) a baseline model (similar and dissimilar trials are not differentiated);  

 
 
 
 
3 

2) a main model (similar and dissimilar trials are modeled separately; inter-trial variability 

was not modeled as is the case in the model reported in the manuscript);  

3) a full model (similar and dissimilar trials are modeled separately; inter-trial variability is 

modeled for the drift rate).  

Finally, we compared the fit for all the models using the deviance information criterion2 

(DIC).  

Experiment 1: Results from our main model showed significant trial similarity differences in  

the drift rate, t(70) = 24.71, p < .001, d = .92, 95% CI = .26, .31 and separation parameters, t(70) = 
2.99, p = .004, d = .47, 95% CI = .07, .34, with the dissimilar condition resulting in greater values 
for both compared to the similar condition (drift: dissimilar, M = .63, SD = .11, similar, M = .34, SD 
= .1; separation: dissimilar, M = 3.42, SD = .74, similar, M = 3.22, SD = .62). There was no evidence 
for statistically significant trial similarity differences in the non-decision parameter (dissimilar, M = 
1.31, SD = .39, similar, M = 1.33, SD = .42, t(69) = -.30, p =.76, d = -.25, 95% CI = -.11, .08). These 
mirrored the results from the main manuscript. 

Next, we compared the fit of the main model to the fit of the baseline model and the full 

model. The main model resulted in the lowest DIC (DIC=5523.52) compared to the baseline model 
(DIC = 5560.54) and the full model (DIC= 5592.5).  

Experiment 2: Results from the main model revealed significant trial similarity differences 

between the drift rate, t(65) = 17.75, p < .001, d = .76, 95% CI = .16, .20 and separation parameters, 
t(66) = 2.15, p = .03, d = .28, 95% CI = .01, .25, with the dissimilar condition resulting in greater 
values for both compared to the similar condition (drift: dissimilar, M = .45, SD = .08, similar, M = 
.28, SD = .07; separation: dissimilar, M = 3.64, SD = .50, similar, M = 3.51, SD = .50). There was no 
evidence for statistically significant differences between the non-decision parameter for the 
dissimilar condition compared to the similar condition (dissimilar, M = 1.23, SD = .42, similar, M = 
1.22, SD = .47, t(65) = .11, p =.91, d = -.03, 95% CI = -.12, .14). These mirrored the results from the 
main manuscript. 

Next, we compared the fit of the main model to the fit of the baseline model and the full 

model. The main model resulted in the lowest DIC (DIC=5853.60) compared to the baseline model 
(DIC = 5885.13) and the full model (DIC= 5928.74).

Within-Image Gaze Transitions  

As a complementary, exploratory analysis to the gaze-switch analyses in the main 

manuscript, we computed the number of gaze transitions within each individual image. We reasoned 
that in more difficult trials potentially associated with more uncertainty (i.e., inaccurate or similar-
accurate trials) toddlers would have more gaze transitions within each image, compared to image 
pairs in the less uncertain trials (dissimilar-accurate trials). For this analysis, we defined 4 
rectangular areas of interest (AOIs) of size 467x118 pixels with slanted inner corners for each 
stimulus at the top, bottom, right, and left side of the occlusion panel shown in Supplementary 
Figure 3. These AOIs were chosen because they contained non occluded visual information needed 
in order to identify the image behind the square. Within-image transitions were computed as the 
number of times toddlers moved their gaze between these rectangular AOIs on each image (i.e., 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
target or lure) separately. Gaze transitions within each rectangular AOI are not counted. The first 
fixation to an AOI was not counted as a transition, so the minimum number of transitions for each 
image was zero. Therefore, we generated two gaze transitions metrics for each trial, namely the 
number of gaze transitions within the target image, and the number of gaze transitions within the 
lure image.

Supplementary Results 4 
Spontaneous Behaviors Indicative of Uncertainty  

Previous research has utilized different behavioral measures in order to gain insight about 

infants’ abilities to track their states of uncertainty.3 We explored whether toddlers in our 
experiments spontaneously produced such behaviors in order to connect our research to previous 
research.  

We trained two naïve coders to code the presence or absence of behavioral expressions of 
uncertainty on each response trial. These behaviors included head turn (towards experimenter or 
parent), child changing their mind (giving a response and then quickly taking it back and giving 
another one), and use of “I don’t know” verbal expression. For each behavior, trials were marked 
either with a 1 (behavior present) or a 0 (behavior not present). The coders were blind to the 
accuracy of the toddlers and to the similarity ratings of the trials. Since the behaviors were relatively 
infrequent (head turn: 3.2% of trials, changing mind: 1.8% of trials and “I don’t know” 1.4% of 
trials), we chose to collapse across experiments and to calculate a composite score. This composite 
score was calculated by summing all three variables. Sixty-six participants in Experiment 1, and 60 
participants in Experiment 2 were coded. Seven participants from Experiment 1 and 1 participant 
from Experiment 2 were not coded due to poor quality or positioning of video. The Intraclass 
Correlation for the composite score was .71, which indicates good level of coding reliability. 

Results. We conducted a one-way (dissimilar-accurate vs. similar-accurate vs. inaccurate) 

repeated measures ANOVA with the composite uncertainty score as the dependent measure in order 
to examine differences in the composite score as a function of trial type. We found a main effect of

.07, SD = .17), t(115) = 2.94, p = .004, d = .27, 95% CI = .01, .07. There was no evidence for 
statistically significant differences between similar-accurate (M = .04, SD = .12) and dissimilar-
accurate or inaccurate trials (t(115) = -.93, p = .36, d = -.09, 95% CI = -.03, .01 and t(115) = 1.91, p 
= .06, d = .18, 95% CI = -.001, .06 respectively).  

Additionally, we explored whether individual differences in our composite score were related 

to variables from the main manuscript. For inaccurate trials, the composite score was positively 
related to response latencies in the touchscreen task (r = .28, p = .004, 95% CI = .10, .44), gaze 
switches (r = .27, p = .003, 95% CI = .09, .43), and the separation parameter (r = .22, p = .03, 95% 
CI = .04, .39).  
These results suggest that the toddlers showed more spontaneous behaviors suggesting uncertainty in 
inaccurate trials compared to the most accurate trials (dissimilar-accurate). In addition, the 
correlation between our composite score and the separation parameter suggests that these implicit 
behaviors may be more strongly associated with how much evidence is needed to endorse a decision 
than evidence accumulation; indeed, there was no evidence for a statistically significant correlation 
between the drift parameter and our composite score (r = -.09, p = .38, 95% CI = -.27, .09).
